Section 10: Linear Equations

In Section 9, we learned to simplify expressions-but an expression by itself is just a recipe for calculation. When we set two expressions equal to each other, we create an equation-a statement that can be true or false depending on the value of the variable. Our job now becomes finding which values make the statement true.

10.1 What Equations Mean

An equation is a statement that two expressions have the same value. The key feature is the equals sign: something on the left equals something on the right.

Equations and Solutions

An equation is a mathematical statement that two expressions are equal.

A solution is a value of the variable that makes the equation true.

To solve an equation is to find all its solutions, or to show that no solutions exist.

Some equations have no solution. Others have exactly one solution, several solutions, or even infinitely many solutions. Part of solving an equation is determining which case applies.

Think of an equation as a balance scale. The expression on the left has a certain "weight" (value), and the expression on the right has a weight too. The equation asserts that these weights are equal-the scale is balanced. When an equation involves a variable, changing the variable's value changes the "weight" on one or both sides. Solving an equation means finding values of the variable that make both sides balance, or determining that no such values exist.

Fig. 10.1: An equation is like a balance scale. Solving means finding the value of the variable that makes both sides balance.

Checking Solutions

Is $x = 3$ a solution to $2x + 1 = 7$?

Substitute: $2(3) + 1 = 6 + 1 = 7$ (matches right side)

Yes, $x = 3$ is a solution.

Is $x = 4$ a solution to $2x + 1 = 7$?

Substitute: $2(4) + 1 = 8 + 1 = 9 \neq 7$

No, $x = 4$ is not a solution.

Is $y = -2$ a solution to $y^2 - 3y = 10$?

Substitute: $(-2)^2 - 3(-2) = 4 + 6 = 10$ (matches)

Yes, $y = -2$ is a solution.

10.2 The Landscape of Equations

Not all equations are created equal. They come in different types, and each type requires different techniques to solve. Before we focus on the simplest type, let's see the broader landscape.

Fig. 10.2: Equations come in different types, each requiring different solution techniques.

Linear equations (like $3x + 5 = 14$) have the variable only to the first power-no $x^2$, no $x^3$, no $x$ in a denominator or under a root. These are the simplest equations and always yield to systematic methods.

Quadratic equations (like $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$) have the variable squared. They can have zero, one, or two solutions. We'll tackle these in Section 17.

Polynomial equations of higher degree (cubic, quartic, etc.) involve $x^3$, $x^4$, and beyond. Their solution methods range from factoring to specialized formulas to numerical approximation.

Transcendental equations involve functions like $\sin$, $\cos$, $\log$, or exponentials. These often require techniques from trigonometry or logarithms-see the companion lessons on those topics.

Differential equations go a step further-they involve not just variables, but how variables change. An equation like $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2y$ relates a quantity to its own rate of change. These equations require entirely new concepts from calculus. See the box below if you're curious about where this leads.

The Bigger Picture: Equations with Rates of Change

The equations above all ask "what value of $x$ makes this true?" But some of the most powerful equations in science describe how things change. A differential equation like $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2y$ says "the rate of change of $y$ is proportional to $y$ itself." Such equations model population growth, radioactive decay, compound interest, and much more.

Solving differential equations requires calculus-specifically, finding functions whose derivatives have specified properties. The companion calculus lesson introduces derivatives (the instantaneous rate of change) and shows how they become mathematical objects in their own right. Understanding linear and quadratic equations first provides essential foundation for that journey.

10.3 Properties of Equality

Before we focus on specific types of equations, let's establish the fundamental rules that apply to all equations. The balance scale metaphor tells us what we can do to an equation without breaking it: whatever we do to one side, we must do to the other to keep the scale balanced.

Properties of Equality

Addition Property: If $a = b$, then $a + c = b + c$

You can add the same quantity to both sides.

Subtraction Property: If $a = b$, then $a - c = b - c$

You can subtract the same quantity from both sides.

Multiplication Property: If $a = b$, then $ac = bc$

You can multiply both sides by the same quantity.

Division Property: If $a = b$ and $c \neq 0$, then $\frac{a}{c} = \frac{b}{c}$

You can divide both sides by the same nonzero quantity.

These properties are universal-they work for linear equations, quadratic equations, and every other type. They're the fundamental tools for equation-solving. Notice that division requires $c \neq 0$-you can never divide by zero.

10.4 Focus: Linear Equations

For the rest of this section, we focus on linear equations-equations where the variable appears only to the first power, not multiplied by itself or inside other functions.

Linear Equation in One Variable

A linear equation in one variable is an equation that can be written in the form

$ax + b = c$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants and $a \neq 0$.

Linear or Not?

$2x + 5 = 11$ - Linear (variable to first power only)

$x^2 = 9$ - Not linear (variable is squared)

$\frac{x}{3} + 4 = 7$ - Linear ($\frac{x}{3} = \frac{1}{3}x$, still first power)

$\frac{5}{x} = 2$ - Not linear (variable in denominator means $x^{-1}$)

$3(x - 2) = x + 4$ - Linear. Since $3(x-2) = 3x - 6$, this equation can be rewritten as $3x - 6 = x + 4$, which simplifies to $2x = 10$. All terms have $x$ to the first power.

$\sqrt{x} = 4$ - Not linear (square root means $x^{1/2}$)

Why start with linear equations? Because they have a wonderful property: there's a systematic method that always works. Every linear equation has exactly one solution (with rare special cases we'll address). And the technique for solving them-using inverse operations to isolate the variable-extends to more complex equations later.

10.5 Solving One-Step Equations

The goal of solving an equation is to isolate the variable-get the variable alone on one side with a number on the other. For one-step equations, a single operation does the job.

The key insight is using inverse operations: operations that undo each other. Addition and subtraction are inverses; multiplication and division are inverses.

One-Step Equations

Solve $x + 8 = 15$:

$x + 8 - 8 = 15 - 8$

$x = 7$

Check: $7 + 8 = 15$ (correct)

Solve $y - 3 = 10$:

$y - 3 + 3 = 10 + 3$

$y = 13$

Check: $13 - 3 = 10$ (correct)

Solve $5x = 35$:

$\frac{5x}{5} = \frac{35}{5}$

$x = 7$

Check: $5(7) = 35$ (correct)

Solve $\frac{n}{4} = 6$:

$\frac{n}{4} \cdot 4 = 6 \cdot 4$

$n = 24$

Check: $\frac{24}{4} = 6$ (correct)

10.6 Solving Two-Step Equations

When an equation requires two operations to solve, we typically undo addition/subtraction first, then multiplication/division. Think of it as "peeling off layers" from the variable.

Two-Step Strategy

  1. Undo addition or subtraction (isolate the term containing the variable)
  2. Undo multiplication or division (isolate the variable itself)

Two-Step Equations

Solve $2x + 5 = 13$:

$2x + 5 - 5 = 13 - 5$ (subtract 5)

$2x = 8$

$x = 4$ (divide by 2)

Check: $2(4) + 5 = 8 + 5 = 13$ (correct)

Solve $\frac{x}{3} - 7 = 2$:

$\frac{x}{3} = 9$ (add 7)

$x = 27$ (multiply by 3)

Check: $\frac{27}{3} - 7 = 9 - 7 = 2$ (correct)

Solve $15 - 4y = 3$:

$-4y = -12$ (subtract 15)

$y = 3$ (divide by $-4$)

Check: $15 - 4(3) = 15 - 12 = 3$ (correct)

10.7 Solving Multi-Step Equations

More complex equations require simplifying each side first, then applying our solving techniques.

Multi-Step Strategy

  1. Simplify each side (distribute, combine like terms)
  2. Collect variable terms on one side
  3. Collect constants on the other side
  4. Isolate the variable (divide by the coefficient)

Equations Requiring Distribution

Solve $3(x + 4) = 21$:

$3x + 12 = 21$ (distribute)

$3x = 9$, so $x = 3$

Check: $3(3 + 4) = 3(7) = 21$ (correct)

Solve $5x + 3 = 2x + 15$:

$3x + 3 = 15$ (subtract $2x$)

$3x = 12$, so $x = 4$

Check: $5(4) + 3 = 23$ and $2(4) + 15 = 23$ (both equal)

Clearing Fractions

Equations with fractions can look intimidating, but there's a systematic approach: multiply every term by a common multiple of the denominators to eliminate all fractions at once. Using the least common denominator (LCD) keeps the numbers smallest, but any common multiple works. A simple approach is to just multiply all the denominators together-it's larger than the LCD, but always works.

Solve $\frac{x}{2} + \frac{x}{3} = 10$:

Multiply by LCD (6): $3x + 2x = 60$

$5x = 60$, so $x = 12$

Check: $\frac{12}{2} + \frac{12}{3} = 6 + 4 = 10$ (correct)

Solve $\frac{2x - 1}{3} = \frac{x + 4}{5}$:

Multiply by LCD (15): $5(2x - 1) = 3(x + 4)$

$10x - 5 = 3x + 12$

$7x = 17$

$x = \frac{17}{7}$

Check: $\frac{2(\frac{17}{7}) - 1}{3} = \frac{\frac{34}{7} - \frac{7}{7}}{3} = \frac{\frac{27}{7}}{3} = \frac{27}{21} = \frac{9}{7}$

And $\frac{\frac{17}{7} + 4}{5} = \frac{\frac{17}{7} + \frac{28}{7}}{5} = \frac{\frac{45}{7}}{5} = \frac{45}{35} = \frac{9}{7}$ (both equal)

Solve $\frac{3x + 2}{4} - \frac{x - 1}{6} = 2$:

Multiply by LCD (12): $3(3x + 2) - 2(x - 1) = 24$

$9x + 6 - 2x + 2 = 24$

$7x + 8 = 24$

$7x = 16$

$x = \frac{16}{7}$

This is approximately $2.286$. Not a "nice" number, but perfectly valid!

Solve $0.4x + 1.5 = 0.6x - 0.3$:

Decimals are just fractions in disguise. Multiply by 10 to clear them:

$4x + 15 = 6x - 3$

$18 = 2x$

$x = 9$

Check: $0.4(9) + 1.5 = 3.6 + 1.5 = 5.1$ and $0.6(9) - 0.3 = 5.4 - 0.3 = 5.1$ (correct)

Don't Expect Nice Numbers!

In real applications, solutions are often fractions or decimals, not convenient integers. The solutions $x = \frac{17}{7}$ or $x = -2.783$ are just as valid as $x = 3$-they simply mean the check requires more work. A non-integer answer isn't a sign that you've made an error.

Some solutions can't even be written exactly as fractions or terminating decimals. Consider the equation $x^2 = 2$. The exact solution is $x = \sqrt{2}$. As we'll see in a later lesson, $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational-its decimal expansion goes on forever without repeating: $1.41421356...$

In practice, an engineer or scientist decides how much precision is needed. The approximation $1.414$ might suffice for rough work, since $1.414^2 = 1.999396$, pretty close to 2. For greater accuracy, you might use $1.41421$, giving $1.41421^2 = 1.9999899...$. No matter how many digits you use, your decimal answer will never be exactly right-but it can be as close as you need.

10.8 Special Cases

Most linear equations have exactly one solution. But two special cases can occur:

Special Cases

No solution (contradiction): The equation simplifies to a false statement like $5 = 3$. No value of the variable works.

Infinitely many solutions (identity): The equation simplifies to a true statement like $5 = 5$. Every value of the variable works.

Recognizing Special Cases

Solve $2(x + 3) = 2x + 5$:

$2x + 6 = 2x + 5$

$6 = 5$ (subtract $2x$)

This is false! No solution.

Solve $3(x - 2) = 3x - 6$:

$3x - 6 = 3x - 6$

$0 = 0$

This is always true! Infinitely many solutions.

10.9 Word Problems and Applications

The real power of algebra is solving problems stated in words. Many students find word problems challenging, but they follow a systematic process.

Translating Word Problems

  1. Read carefully and identify what you're looking for
  2. Define a variable: "Let $x$ = ..." (be specific about units!)
  3. Translate the relationships into an equation
  4. Solve the equation
  5. Check your answer against the original problem (not just the equation)
  6. State your answer with appropriate units and precision

Number Problems

"Three more than twice a number is 17. Find the number."

Let $n$ = the number. Equation: $2n + 3 = 17$

$2n = 14$, so $n = 7$

Check: Three more than twice 7 is $2(7) + 3 = 17$ (correct)

"When a number is increased by 20% of itself, the result is 54. Find the number."

Let $n$ = the number. "Increased by 20% of itself" means $n + 0.20n = 1.20n$

Equation: $1.20n = 54$

$n = \frac{54}{1.20} = 45$

Check: $45 + 0.20(45) = 45 + 9 = 54$ (correct)

Geometry Problems

"A rectangle's length is 4 more than its width. The perimeter is 56 cm. Find the dimensions."

Let $w$ = width. Length $= w + 4$

Perimeter: $2w + 2(w + 4) = 56$

$4w + 8 = 56$, so $4w = 48$, $w = 12$

Width = 12 cm, Length = 16 cm

"A triangle has a perimeter of 50 inches. The second side is twice the first side, and the third side is 5 inches longer than the first. Find all three sides."

Let $x$ = first side. Second side $= 2x$. Third side $= x + 5$.

Equation: $x + 2x + (x + 5) = 50$

$4x + 5 = 50$

$4x = 45$

$x = \frac{45}{4} = 11.25$ inches

First side: 11.25 in, Second side: 22.5 in, Third side: 16.25 in

Check: $11.25 + 22.5 + 16.25 = 50$ (correct)

"A circular garden has a circumference of 25 feet. What is its diameter?"

Let $d$ = diameter. Circumference formula: $C = \pi d$

Equation: $\pi d = 25$

$d = \frac{25}{\pi}$

This is an exact answer, but since $\pi$ is irrational, the decimal never terminates:

$d \approx 7.96$ feet (rounded to two decimal places)

$d \approx 7.958$ feet (to three decimal places)

Note: $\frac{25}{\pi}$ is the exact answer. The decimal approximations are for practical use.

Money and Business Problems

"A store marks up items by 35%. If a shirt sells for 48.60 dollars, what was the wholesale cost?"

Let $c$ = wholesale cost. After 35% markup: $c + 0.35c = 1.35c$

Equation: $1.35c = 48.60$

$c = \frac{48.60}{1.35} = 36$

Calculator tip: Just compute $48.60 \div 1.35$. Or simplify the fraction: $\frac{48.60}{1.35} = \frac{4860}{135} = \frac{972}{27}$ (divide by 5) $= \frac{324}{9}$ (divide by 3) $= 36$.

Wholesale cost: 36.00 dollars

Check: $36 + 0.35(36) = 36 + 12.60 = 48.60$ (correct)

"You invest a total of 5,000 dollars in two accounts. One pays 4% annual interest, the other pays 6%. After one year, your total interest is 248 dollars. How much did you invest in each account?"

Let $x$ = amount at 4%. Then $5000 - x$ = amount at 6%.

Interest equation: $0.04x + 0.06(5000 - x) = 248$

$0.04x + 300 - 0.06x = 248$

$-0.02x = -52$

$x = 2600$

Answer: 2,600 dollars at 4%, 2,400 dollars at 6%

Check: $0.04(2600) + 0.06(2400) = 104 + 144 = 248$ (correct)

Mixture and Rate Problems

"How many liters of a 40% acid solution must be mixed with 8 liters of a 70% acid solution to produce a 50% acid solution?"

Let $x$ = liters of 40% solution. Note: The final mixture will contain $x + 8$ liters total.

Acid content: (acid in 40%) + (acid in 70%) = (acid in mixture)

$0.40x + 0.70(8) = 0.50(x + 8)$

$0.40x + 5.6 = 0.50x + 4$

$1.6 = 0.10x$

$x = 16$ liters

Check: $0.40(16) + 0.70(8) = 6.4 + 5.6 = 12$ liters of acid

Total mixture: $16 + 8 = 24$ liters. Concentration: $\frac{12}{24} = 0.50 = 50\%$ (correct)

"A car travels at 55 mph for part of a trip and 70 mph for the rest. The total trip is 180 miles and takes 3 hours. How long was each part?"

Let $t$ = time at 55 mph (in hours). Then $3 - t$ = time at 70 mph.

Distance equation: $55t + 70(3 - t) = 180$

$55t + 210 - 70t = 180$

$-15t = -30$

$t = 2$ hours

Answer: 2 hours at 55 mph, 1 hour at 70 mph

Check: $55(2) + 70(1) = 110 + 70 = 180$ miles (correct)

Real-World Answers Aren't Always "Nice"

In textbook problems, answers are often chosen to come out as integers. In real applications, expect decimals, fractions, and sometimes irrational numbers. A measurement of $11.25$ inches or $\frac{25}{\pi}$ feet is just as valid as $12$ inches. When an answer involves $\pi$, $\sqrt{2}$, or similar constants, you may need to:

  • Give the exact answer (like $\frac{25}{\pi}$) for mathematical precision
  • Give a decimal approximation (like $7.96$ feet) for practical use
  • State the precision (how many decimal places) appropriate to the situation

Equations appear constantly in programming. When you write if score + bonus == 100, you're checking whether an equation is satisfied. Game physics, financial calculations, and data validation all involve setting up and solving equations-often the same kinds of linear equations we've been studying, just computed thousands of times per second.

The word "algebra" comes from al-jabr, meaning "restoration" or "completion," from the title of a 9th-century book by Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi. His name also gave us the word "algorithm." Al-Khwarizmi's book systematically solved linear and quadratic equations-not with our modern notation (that came centuries later), but with verbal recipes. The systematic approach-identify the equation type, apply the appropriate method-is essentially what we still do today.

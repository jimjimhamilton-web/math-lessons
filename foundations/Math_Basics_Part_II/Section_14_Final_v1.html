Section 14: Polynomials

In Section 9, we learned to work with algebraic expressions—combinations of numbers, variables, and operations. We saw how to evaluate expressions by substituting values, and how to simplify them by combining like terms. Now we give a formal name to an important class of expressions that appears throughout mathematics: polynomials.

Polynomials are the building blocks of algebra. They show up in area and volume calculations, in physics equations describing motion, in economics models, and in countless other applications. Learning to work with polynomials fluently—adding, subtracting, multiplying, and eventually factoring them—opens the door to solving a much wider range of problems than we could tackle with linear equations alone.

14.1 What Is a Polynomial?

Polynomial

A polynomial is an expression that is a sum of terms, where each term is a product of a numerical coefficient and variables raised to non-negative integer powers.

More simply: a polynomial is built from numbers and variables using only addition, subtraction, and multiplication—no division by variables, no square roots of variables, no variables in exponents.

Here are some examples of polynomials:

  • $3x + 5$
  • $x^2 - 4x + 7$
  • $2y^3 + y^2 - 3y + 1$
  • $5$ (a constant is a polynomial with no variable part)
  • $x$ (a single variable is a polynomial)
  • $x^2y + 3xy^2 - 2xy$ (polynomials can have multiple variables)

And here are some expressions that are not polynomials:

  • $\dfrac{1}{x}$ or $x^{-1}$ (negative exponent means division by $x$)
  • $\sqrt{x}$ or $x^{1/2}$ (fractional exponent)
  • $2^x$ (variable in the exponent)
  • $\dfrac{x+1}{x-2}$ (variable in the denominator)

Anatomy of a Polynomial

Let's dissect a polynomial to understand its parts. Consider:

$5x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x - 4$

Terms are the pieces being added or subtracted. This polynomial has four terms: $5x^3$, $-2x^2$, $7x$, and $-4$. Notice that we include the sign with each term—the second term is $-2x^2$, not $2x^2$.

Coefficients are the numerical factors in each term. The coefficients here are $5$, $-2$, $7$, and $-4$. The coefficient of $x^3$ is $5$; the coefficient of $x^2$ is $-2$.

Degree of a term is the sum of the exponents on the variables in that term. In a single-variable polynomial, this is just the exponent on the variable:

  • $5x^3$ has degree 3
  • $-2x^2$ has degree 2
  • $7x$ has degree 1 (since $x = x^1$)
  • $-4$ has degree 0 (since $-4 = -4x^0$)

Degree of a polynomial is the highest degree among all its terms. The polynomial $5x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x - 4$ has degree 3.

Leading term is the term with the highest degree. Here, it's $5x^3$.

Leading coefficient is the coefficient of the leading term. Here, it's $5$.

Constant term is the term with no variable (degree 0). Here, it's $-4$.

Standard Form

A polynomial is in standard form when its terms are written in descending order of degree (highest power first). The polynomial $5x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x - 4$ is already in standard form.

The polynomial $3 + 2x^2 - x$ is not in standard form. Rewriting it in standard form gives: $2x^2 - x + 3$.

Classifying Polynomials

Polynomials are often classified by how many terms they have:

Name Number of Terms Examples
Monomial 1 $5x^3$,   $-2y$,   $7$
Binomial 2 $x + 3$,   $2x^2 - 5x$,   $a^2 - b^2$
Trinomial 3 $x^2 + 2x + 1$,   $3a - 2b + c$
Polynomial Any number (general term for all of these)

Polynomials are also classified by their degree:

Degree Name Examples
0 Constant $7$,   $-3$,   $\frac{1}{2}$
1 Linear $3x + 2$,   $-y + 5$
2 Quadratic $x^2 - 4x + 3$,   $2y^2 + 1$
3 Cubic $x^3 + x^2 - x + 1$
4 Quartic $x^4 - 1$
5 Quintic $x^5 + 2x^3 - x$

The linear equations we studied in Sections 10–13 all involved polynomials of degree 1. In Section 16, we'll focus specifically on quadratic equations (degree 2), which have rich properties and many applications.

The word "polynomial" combines Greek poly- ("many") with Latin nomen ("name" or "term")—literally "many terms."

The word "algebra" has a more specific origin. Around 830 CE, the Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing"). The Arabic word al-jabr ("completion" or "restoration") in the title became "algebra" when the book was translated into Latin in the 12th century.

Al-Khwarizmi worked at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad and systematized methods for solving equations that had been developed over centuries by Babylonian, Greek, and Indian mathematicians. His book was practical—focused on inheritance calculations, land division, and trade—and became the standard algebra text in European universities for centuries.

His name also gave us another word: "algorithm." A different book of his, on Hindu-Arabic numerals, was titled Algoritmi de numero Indorum in Latin, and "Algoritmi" evolved into our modern term for step-by-step procedures.

The history of algebra is explored more fully in the Math History appendix.

Polynomials with Multiple Variables

So far, most of our examples have used a single variable. Polynomials can also involve multiple variables:

$3x^2y + 2xy^2 - 5xy + 4$

For multi-variable polynomials, the degree of a term is the sum of all the exponents:

  • $3x^2y$ has degree $2 + 1 = 3$
  • $2xy^2$ has degree $1 + 2 = 3$
  • $-5xy$ has degree $1 + 1 = 2$
  • $4$ has degree $0$

The degree of this polynomial, $3x^2y + 2xy^2 - 5xy + 4$, is 3 (the highest term degree).

Practice: Polynomial Terminology

1. For the polynomial $4x^3 - 7x^2 + 2x - 9$:

(a) How many terms does it have?

(b) What is the coefficient of $x^2$?

(c) What is the degree of the polynomial?

(d) What is the leading coefficient?

(e) What is the constant term?

Solution:

(a) Four terms: $4x^3$, $-7x^2$, $2x$, and $-9$

(b) The coefficient of $x^2$ is $-7$ (including the negative sign)

(c) Degree 3 (the highest exponent)

(d) Leading coefficient is $4$ (the coefficient of the highest-degree term)

(e) Constant term is $-9$

2. Classify each polynomial by number of terms (monomial, binomial, trinomial) and by degree:

(a) $5x^2 - 3$

(b) $7y^4$

(c) $x^2 + 4x - 1$

(d) $8$

Solution:

(a) Binomial (2 terms), quadratic (degree 2)

(b) Monomial (1 term), quartic (degree 4)

(c) Trinomial (3 terms), quadratic (degree 2)

(d) Monomial (1 term), constant (degree 0)

3. Write each polynomial in standard form:

(a) $3 - 2x + x^2$

(b) $5x - x^3 + 2x^2 - 1$

(c) $y + 4y^3 - 2y^2$

Solution:

(a) $x^2 - 2x + 3$

(b) $-x^3 + 2x^2 + 5x - 1$

(c) $4y^3 - 2y^2 + y$

4. Which of the following are polynomials? For those that are not, explain why.

(a) $x^3 + 2x - \sqrt{5}$

(b) $\dfrac{3}{x} + x^2$

(c) $4x^{-2} + x$

(d) $\sqrt{x} + 1$

Solution:

(a) Yes, this is a polynomial. The $\sqrt{5}$ is just a constant coefficient (approximately 2.236); the variables have only non-negative integer exponents.

(b) No. The term $\frac{3}{x} = 3x^{-1}$ has a negative exponent, which means division by $x$.

(c) No. The term $4x^{-2}$ has a negative exponent.

(d) No. The term $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$ has a fractional exponent.

5. Find the degree of each term and the degree of the polynomial $2x^2y^3 - 5xy + 3x - 7$.

Solution:

Term $2x^2y^3$: degree = $2 + 3 = 5$

Term $-5xy$: degree = $1 + 1 = 2$

Term $3x$: degree = $1$

Term $-7$: degree = $0$

Degree of polynomial: 5 (the highest term degree)

14.2 Adding Polynomials

Adding polynomials is something we already know how to do—it's combining like terms, which we learned in Section 9.4. The key insight remains the same: we can only combine terms that have exactly the same variable parts (the same variables raised to the same powers).

Adding Polynomials

To add polynomials, combine like terms by adding their coefficients. Terms are "like" if they have exactly the same variable parts.

Like terms: $3x^2$ and $-5x^2$;   $7xy$ and $2xy$

Unlike terms: $3x^2$ and $3x$ (different powers);   $xy$ and $x^2y$ (different variable parts)

There are two common ways to organize the work: the horizontal method and the vertical method.

Horizontal Method

Write the polynomials side by side, remove parentheses, and combine like terms.

Example 1: Adding Binomials

Add: $(3x + 5) + (2x + 4)$

Step 1: Remove parentheses (nothing changes because we're adding):

$3x + 5 + 2x + 4$

Step 2: Group like terms:

$(3x + 2x) + (5 + 4)$

Step 3: Combine:

$5x + 9$

Answer: $5x + 9$

Example 2: Adding Trinomials

Add: $(x^2 + 3x + 2) + (2x^2 - x + 5)$

Remove parentheses:

$x^2 + 3x + 2 + 2x^2 - x + 5$

Group like terms:

$(x^2 + 2x^2) + (3x - x) + (2 + 5)$

Combine:

$3x^2 + 2x + 7$

Answer: $3x^2 + 2x + 7$

Example 3: Polynomials with "Missing" Terms

Add: $(4x^3 - 2x + 7) + (x^2 + 3x - 4)$

The first polynomial has no $x^2$ term; the second has no $x^3$ term. That's fine—we just combine what we can.

$4x^3 - 2x + 7 + x^2 + 3x - 4$

Group like terms (the $x^3$ and $x^2$ terms have no partners):

$4x^3 + x^2 + (-2x + 3x) + (7 - 4)$

Combine:

$4x^3 + x^2 + x + 3$

Answer: $4x^3 + x^2 + x + 3$

Vertical Method

Align like terms in columns and add down each column, similar to adding multi-digit numbers.

Example 4: Adding with the Vertical Method

Add: $(3x^2 + 5x - 2) + (x^2 - 3x + 7)$

Align like terms: 

    3x² + 5x - 2
+   x² - 3x + 7
─────────────────
    4x² + 2x + 5

Answer: $4x^2 + 2x + 5$

The vertical method is especially helpful when adding polynomials with many terms, or when some terms are "missing"—you can leave blanks in the appropriate columns.

Example 5: Adding Polynomials with Fractions

Add: $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}x^2 + \dfrac{1}{3}x\right) + \left(\dfrac{1}{4}x^2 - \dfrac{2}{3}x\right)$

Group like terms:

$\left(\dfrac{1}{2}x^2 + \dfrac{1}{4}x^2\right) + \left(\dfrac{1}{3}x - \dfrac{2}{3}x\right)$

Add the $x^2$ coefficients: $\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{2}{4} + \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{3}{4}$

Add the $x$ coefficients: $\dfrac{1}{3} - \dfrac{2}{3} = -\dfrac{1}{3}$

Answer: $\dfrac{3}{4}x^2 - \dfrac{1}{3}x$

Practice: Adding Polynomials

6. Add: $(5x + 7) + (3x - 2)$

Solution:

$5x + 7 + 3x - 2$

$= (5x + 3x) + (7 - 2)$

$= 8x + 5$

7. Add: $(2x^2 - 4x + 1) + (x^2 + 5x - 3)$

Solution:

$2x^2 - 4x + 1 + x^2 + 5x - 3$

$= (2x^2 + x^2) + (-4x + 5x) + (1 - 3)$

$= 3x^2 + x - 2$

8. Add: $(x^3 + 2x) + (3x^2 - x + 4)$

Solution:

$x^3 + 2x + 3x^2 - x + 4$

$= x^3 + 3x^2 + (2x - x) + 4$

$= x^3 + 3x^2 + x + 4$

9. Add: $(4y^3 - y^2 + 2y) + (-2y^3 + 3y^2 - y + 5)$

Solution:

$4y^3 - y^2 + 2y - 2y^3 + 3y^2 - y + 5$

$= (4y^3 - 2y^3) + (-y^2 + 3y^2) + (2y - y) + 5$

$= 2y^3 + 2y^2 + y + 5$

10. Add: $\left(\dfrac{2}{3}x^2 - \dfrac{1}{2}x + 1\right) + \left(\dfrac{1}{3}x^2 + \dfrac{3}{2}x - 4\right)$

Solution:

$x^2$ terms: $\dfrac{2}{3} + \dfrac{1}{3} = 1$

$x$ terms: $-\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{3}{2} = \dfrac{2}{2} = 1$

Constants: $1 - 4 = -3$

Answer: $x^2 + x - 3$

14.3 Subtracting Polynomials

Subtracting polynomials requires one additional step: we must distribute the subtraction (the minus sign) to every term in the polynomial being subtracted. This changes the sign of each term in that polynomial.

Subtracting Polynomials

To subtract a polynomial, add its opposite. That is, change the sign of every term in the polynomial being subtracted, then combine like terms.

$A - B = A + (-B)$

Common Error: Forgetting to Distribute the Minus Sign

The most frequent mistake when subtracting polynomials is changing the sign of only the first term instead of all terms.

Wrong: $(3x^2 + 2x) - (x^2 + 5x) = 3x^2 + 2x - x^2 + 5x$   ✗

Right: $(3x^2 + 2x) - (x^2 + 5x) = 3x^2 + 2x - x^2 - 5x$   ✓

In the wrong version, only the $x^2$ got its sign changed. The $5x$ should also become $-5x$.

Example 6: Subtracting Binomials

Subtract: $(5x + 3) - (2x + 1)$

Step 1: Distribute the minus sign to both terms in the second polynomial:

$5x + 3 - 2x - 1$

Step 2: Combine like terms:

$(5x - 2x) + (3 - 1) = 3x + 2$

Answer: $3x + 2$

Example 7: Subtracting Trinomials

Subtract: $(x^2 + 4x - 3) - (2x^2 - x + 5)$

Distribute the minus sign (every sign in the second polynomial changes):

$x^2 + 4x - 3 - 2x^2 + x - 5$

Combine like terms:

$(x^2 - 2x^2) + (4x + x) + (-3 - 5)$

$= -x^2 + 5x - 8$

Answer: $-x^2 + 5x - 8$

Example 8: Subtracting with Missing Terms

Subtract: $(3x^3 + x) - (x^3 + 2x^2 - 4x + 1)$

Distribute the minus sign to all four terms in the second polynomial:

$3x^3 + x - x^3 - 2x^2 + 4x - 1$

Combine like terms:

$(3x^3 - x^3) - 2x^2 + (x + 4x) - 1$

$= 2x^3 - 2x^2 + 5x - 1$

Answer: $2x^3 - 2x^2 + 5x - 1$

Vertical Method for Subtraction

When using the vertical method, you can either change all signs in the second polynomial before aligning, or align first and then subtract column by column.

Example 9: Vertical Subtraction

Subtract: $(4x^2 + 3x - 5) - (x^2 + 6x - 2)$

Method: Change signs, then add: 

    4x² + 3x - 5
-  (x² + 6x - 2)
─────────────────

Change signs in the bottom polynomial:

    4x² + 3x - 5
+  -x² - 6x + 2
─────────────────
    3x² - 3x - 3

Answer: $3x^2 - 3x - 3$

Example 10: Subtracting Polynomials with Decimals

Subtract: $(2.5x^2 + 1.8x) - (0.7x^2 + 3.2x)$

Distribute the minus sign:

$2.5x^2 + 1.8x - 0.7x^2 - 3.2x$

Combine like terms:

$(2.5 - 0.7)x^2 + (1.8 - 3.2)x$

$= 1.8x^2 - 1.4x$

Answer: $1.8x^2 - 1.4x$

Subtracting in Either Order

Unlike addition, subtraction is not commutative: $A - B \neq B - A$ in general. If you reverse the order, you get the opposite result.

Example 11: Order Matters

Compare $(3x + 5) - (x + 2)$ with $(x + 2) - (3x + 5)$.

First way: $(3x + 5) - (x + 2) = 3x + 5 - x - 2 = 2x + 3$

Second way: $(x + 2) - (3x + 5) = x + 2 - 3x - 5 = -2x - 3$

The answers are opposites: $2x + 3$ and $-(2x + 3) = -2x - 3$.

Practice: Subtracting Polynomials

11. Subtract: $(7x + 4) - (3x + 1)$

Solution:

$7x + 4 - 3x - 1$

$= (7x - 3x) + (4 - 1)$

$= 4x + 3$

12. Subtract: $(4x^2 - 2x + 5) - (x^2 + 3x - 2)$

Solution:

$4x^2 - 2x + 5 - x^2 - 3x + 2$

$= (4x^2 - x^2) + (-2x - 3x) + (5 + 2)$

$= 3x^2 - 5x + 7$

13. Subtract: $(5x^3 - 3x) - (2x^3 - x^2 + x - 4)$

Solution:

$5x^3 - 3x - 2x^3 + x^2 - x + 4$

$= (5x^3 - 2x^3) + x^2 + (-3x - x) + 4$

$= 3x^3 + x^2 - 4x + 4$

14. Subtract: $(6y^2 - y + 3) - (6y^2 + 2y - 1)$

Solution:

$6y^2 - y + 3 - 6y^2 - 2y + 1$

$= (6y^2 - 6y^2) + (-y - 2y) + (3 + 1)$

$= 0 - 3y + 4$

$= -3y + 4$

(Note: The $y^2$ terms cancelled out, leaving a linear polynomial.)

15. Subtract: $\left(\dfrac{3}{4}x^2 + 2x\right) - \left(\dfrac{1}{4}x^2 + \dfrac{5}{2}x\right)$

Solution:

$x^2$ terms: $\dfrac{3}{4} - \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$

$x$ terms: $2 - \dfrac{5}{2} = \dfrac{4}{2} - \dfrac{5}{2} = -\dfrac{1}{2}$

Answer: $\dfrac{1}{2}x^2 - \dfrac{1}{2}x$

16. Subtract: $(1.5a^2 + 3.2a - 0.5) - (2.1a^2 - 1.4a + 2.5)$

Solution:

$1.5a^2 + 3.2a - 0.5 - 2.1a^2 + 1.4a - 2.5$

$a^2$ terms: $1.5 - 2.1 = -0.6$

$a$ terms: $3.2 + 1.4 = 4.6$

Constants: $-0.5 - 2.5 = -3.0$

Answer: $-0.6a^2 + 4.6a - 3$

Combined Operations

Sometimes you'll need to both add and subtract polynomials in the same problem. Work left to right, being careful with signs.

Example 12: Combined Addition and Subtraction

Simplify: $(2x^2 + 3x) + (x^2 - 5x + 1) - (4x^2 - x)$

Handle the addition first (no sign changes), then the subtraction (change signs):

$2x^2 + 3x + x^2 - 5x + 1 - 4x^2 + x$

Combine like terms:

$(2x^2 + x^2 - 4x^2) + (3x - 5x + x) + 1$

$= -x^2 - x + 1$

Answer: $-x^2 - x + 1$

Practice: Combined Operations

17. Simplify: $(3x^2 - x) - (x^2 + 2x) + (4x - 5)$

Solution:

$3x^2 - x - x^2 - 2x + 4x - 5$

$= (3x^2 - x^2) + (-x - 2x + 4x) - 5$

$= 2x^2 + x - 5$

18. Simplify: $(y^3 + 2y) - (y^3 - y^2 + y) + (3y^2 - y + 1)$

Solution:

$y^3 + 2y - y^3 + y^2 - y + 3y^2 - y + 1$

$= (y^3 - y^3) + (y^2 + 3y^2) + (2y - y - y) + 1$

$= 0 + 4y^2 + 0 + 1$

$= 4y^2 + 1$

14.4 Multiplying Polynomials

Multiplying polynomials uses the distributive property—the same principle we've been using since Section 9. The key idea: every term in the first polynomial multiplies every term in the second polynomial.

Multiplying Polynomials

To multiply two polynomials, multiply each term of the first polynomial by each term of the second polynomial, then combine like terms.

If the first polynomial has $m$ terms and the second has $n$ terms, you'll have $m \times n$ products before combining.

Monomial × Polynomial

The simplest case is multiplying a monomial (single term) by a polynomial. This is just the distributive property applied directly.

Example 1: Monomial Times Binomial

Multiply: $3x(x + 4)$

Distribute $3x$ to each term inside the parentheses:

$3x \cdot x + 3x \cdot 4 = 3x^2 + 12x$

Answer: $3x^2 + 12x$

Example 2: Monomial Times Trinomial

Multiply: $-2x^2(x^2 - 3x + 5)$

Distribute $-2x^2$ to each term:

$-2x^2 \cdot x^2 + (-2x^2) \cdot (-3x) + (-2x^2) \cdot 5$

$= -2x^4 + 6x^3 - 10x^2$

Answer: $-2x^4 + 6x^3 - 10x^2$

Binomial × Binomial

When multiplying two binomials, each of the two terms in the first binomial must multiply each of the two terms in the second binomial. That gives $2 \times 2 = 4$ products.

Example 3: Binomial Times Binomial (Full Distribution)

Multiply: $(x + 3)(x + 5)$

First term of $(x + 3)$ times both terms of $(x + 5)$:

$x \cdot x + x \cdot 5 = x^2 + 5x$

Second term of $(x + 3)$ times both terms of $(x + 5)$:

$3 \cdot x + 3 \cdot 5 = 3x + 15$

Add all four products:

$x^2 + 5x + 3x + 15 = x^2 + 8x + 15$

Answer: $x^2 + 8x + 15$

The FOIL Mnemonic

For binomial × binomial specifically, some people use the mnemonic FOIL to remember the four products:

  • First terms: $x \cdot x = x^2$
  • Outer terms: $x \cdot 5 = 5x$
  • Inner terms: $3 \cdot x = 3x$
  • Last terms: $3 \cdot 5 = 15$

Important: FOIL is just a memory aid for the four products in a binomial × binomial. It's not a separate method—it's the distributive property organized in a particular order. If you forget FOIL, you can always fall back on "every term times every term."

Limitation: FOIL only works for binomial × binomial. For larger products, you must use the general distribution method.

Example 4: Binomial with Mixed Signs

Multiply: $(x - 3)(x + 5)$

Using distribution (or FOIL):

$x \cdot x + x \cdot 5 + (-3) \cdot x + (-3) \cdot 5$

$= x^2 + 5x - 3x - 15$

$= x^2 + 2x - 15$

Answer: $x^2 + 2x - 15$

Example 5: Both Binomials with Subtraction

Multiply: $(x - 4)(x - 2)$

$x \cdot x + x \cdot (-2) + (-4) \cdot x + (-4) \cdot (-2)$

$= x^2 - 2x - 4x + 8$

$= x^2 - 6x + 8$

Answer: $x^2 - 6x + 8$

Example 6: Non-Unit Leading Coefficients

Multiply: $(2x + 1)(3x - 4)$

$2x \cdot 3x + 2x \cdot (-4) + 1 \cdot 3x + 1 \cdot (-4)$

$= 6x^2 - 8x + 3x - 4$

$= 6x^2 - 5x - 4$

Answer: $6x^2 - 5x - 4$

Larger Products

For products larger than binomial × binomial, the same principle applies: every term times every term. FOIL doesn't work here—you need the general approach.

Example 7: Binomial Times Trinomial

Multiply: $(x + 2)(x^2 - 3x + 1)$

The first polynomial has 2 terms; the second has 3 terms. That's $2 \times 3 = 6$ products.

$x$ times each term of $(x^2 - 3x + 1)$:

$x \cdot x^2 + x \cdot (-3x) + x \cdot 1 = x^3 - 3x^2 + x$

$2$ times each term of $(x^2 - 3x + 1)$:

$2 \cdot x^2 + 2 \cdot (-3x) + 2 \cdot 1 = 2x^2 - 6x + 2$

Add all products:

$x^3 - 3x^2 + x + 2x^2 - 6x + 2$

Combine like terms:

$x^3 + (-3x^2 + 2x^2) + (x - 6x) + 2 = x^3 - x^2 - 5x + 2$

Answer: $x^3 - x^2 - 5x + 2$

Example 8: Binomial with Fractions

Multiply: $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}x + 3\right)(4x - 2)$

$\dfrac{1}{2}x \cdot 4x + \dfrac{1}{2}x \cdot (-2) + 3 \cdot 4x + 3 \cdot (-2)$

$= 2x^2 - x + 12x - 6$

$= 2x^2 + 11x - 6$

Answer: $2x^2 + 11x - 6$

$x^2 + 3x - 5x - 15$

Add: $x^3 + x^2 + x - x^2 - x - 1$

$= x^3 - 1$

(Note: This is an example of a "difference of cubes" factoring pattern in reverse!)

8. Multiply: $(2y + 3)(y^2 - 2y + 4)$

Solution:

$2y$ times each term: $2y^3 - 4y^2 + 8y$

$3$ times each term: $3y^2 - 6y + 12$

Add: $2y^3 - 4y^2 + 8y + 3y^2 - 6y + 12$

$= 2y^3 + (-4y^2 + 3y^2) + (8y - 6y) + 12$

$= 2y^3 - y^2 + 2y + 12$

14.5 Special Products

Some polynomial products occur so frequently that it's worth memorizing their patterns. Recognizing these special products serves two purposes: it speeds up multiplication, and—more importantly—it prepares us for factoring in Section 15, where we'll reverse these patterns.

Why memorize these patterns?

  • Speed: Once you recognize a pattern, you can write the answer directly without multiplying out all the terms.
  • Factoring: In Section 15, we'll reverse these patterns. When you see $x^2 - 9$, you'll recognize it as $(x + 3)(x - 3)$. When you see $x^2 + 6x + 9$, you'll recognize it as $(x + 3)^2$.
  • Ubiquity: These patterns appear constantly in calculus, physics, engineering, and computer science.

Special Product 1: Square of a Sum

Square of a Sum

$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$

Let's verify this by multiplying it out:

$(a + b)^2 = (a + b)(a + b)$

$= a \cdot a + a \cdot b + b \cdot a + b \cdot b$

$= a^2 + ab + ab + b^2$

$= a^2 + 2ab + b^2$

The middle term $2ab$ comes from the two "cross products" ($a \cdot b$ and $b \cdot a$). This pattern has a beautiful geometric interpretation.

Fig. 14.1 — Area Model for $(a + b)^2$
A square with side $(a + b)$ has total area $(a + b)^2$. Dividing it into four regions shows that this equals $a^2 + ab + ab + b^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$.

Critical Error: $(a + b)^2 \neq a^2 + b^2$

A very common mistake is to "distribute" the exponent and write $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$. This is wrong. The middle term $2ab$ is essential.

Numerical check: Let $a = 3$ and $b = 4$.

  • Correct: $(3 + 4)^2 = 7^2 = 49$
  • Using the formula: $3^2 + 2(3)(4) + 4^2 = 9 + 24 + 16 = 49$   ✓
  • Wrong way: $3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25 \neq 49$   ✗

Remember: Exponents do not distribute over addition. Squaring means multiplying $(a + b)$ by itself, which produces cross-product terms.

Example 9: Square of a Sum

Expand: $(x + 5)^2$

Using the pattern $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ with $a = x$ and $b = 5$:

$(x)^2 + 2(x)(5) + (5)^2 = x^2 + 10x + 25$

Answer: $x^2 + 10x + 25$

Example 10: Square of a Sum with Coefficient

Expand: $(2x + 3)^2$

Here $a = 2x$ and $b = 3$:

$(2x)^2 + 2(2x)(3) + (3)^2 = 4x^2 + 12x + 9$

Answer: $4x^2 + 12x + 9$

Special Product 2: Square of a Difference

Square of a Difference

$(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$

This is the same pattern as the square of a sum, except the middle term is negative (because $(-b) + (-b) = -2b$). Note that the last term is still $+b^2$ because $(-b)^2 = +b^2$.

Example 11: Square of a Difference

Expand: $(x - 4)^2$

Using $(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$ with $a = x$ and $b = 4$:

$(x)^2 - 2(x)(4) + (4)^2 = x^2 - 8x + 16$

Answer: $x^2 - 8x + 16$

Example 12: Square of a Difference with Coefficient

Expand: $(3x - 2)^2$

Here $a = 3x$ and $b = 2$:

$(3x)^2 - 2(3x)(2) + (2)^2 = 9x^2 - 12x + 4$

Answer: $9x^2 - 12x + 4$

Special Product 3: Difference of Squares

Difference of Squares

$(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$

When you multiply the sum and difference of the same two terms, the middle terms cancel:

$(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - ab + ab - b^2 = a^2 - b^2$

The result is remarkably simple: just the difference of the squares, with no middle term at all. This pattern also has a geometric interpretation, though it requires a bit more visualization.

Fig. 14.2 — Area Model for $(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$
(a) Start with square $a^2$
(b) Remove $b^2$; cut along dashed line
(c) Move top piece to form rectangle
The L-shaped region with area $a^2 - b^2$ can be cut and rearranged into a rectangle with dimensions $(a + b)$ by $(a - b)$.

The geometry shows why the formula works: the area of the L-shaped region (what remains after removing a $b \times b$ square from an $a \times a$ square) can be rearranged into a rectangle with dimensions $(a + b)$ by $(a - b)$.

In Section 15, we'll use this pattern in reverse: when you see an expression like $x^2 - 25$, you'll recognize it as a difference of squares and immediately write $(x + 5)(x - 5)$.

Example 13: Difference of Squares

Multiply: $(x + 6)(x - 6)$

Using $(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$ with $a = x$ and $b = 6$:

$x^2 - 6^2 = x^2 - 36$

Answer: $x^2 - 36$

Example 14: Difference of Squares with Coefficients

Multiply: $(3x + 4)(3x - 4)$

Here $a = 3x$ and $b = 4$:

$(3x)^2 - (4)^2 = 9x^2 - 16$

Answer: $9x^2 - 16$

Example 15: Difference of Squares with Two Variables

Multiply: $(2x + 5y)(2x - 5y)$

Here $a = 2x$ and $b = 5y$:

$(2x)^2 - (5y)^2 = 4x^2 - 25y^2$

Answer: $4x^2 - 25y^2$

Recognizing Special Products

Part of mastering these patterns is recognizing when they apply. Not every product is a special product—but when one is, you can save time by using the pattern directly.

Example 16: Which Pattern Applies?

For each product, identify if it's a special product (and which one) or requires general multiplication:

(a) $(x + 7)^2$

(b) $(x + 7)(x - 7)$

(c) $(x + 7)(x + 3)$

(d) $(x - 5)^2$

Solutions:

(a) Square of a sum: $(x + 7)^2 = x^2 + 2(x)(7) + 7^2 = x^2 + 14x + 49$

(b) Difference of squares: $(x + 7)(x - 7) = x^2 - 7^2 = x^2 - 49$

(c) Not a special product (the binomials are different). Use distribution/FOIL:

$x \cdot x + x \cdot 3 + 7 \cdot x + 7 \cdot 3 = x^2 + 3x + 7x + 21 = x^2 + 10x + 21$

(d) Square of a difference: $(x - 5)^2 = x^2 - 2(x)(5) + 5^2 = x^2 - 10x + 25$

Practice: Special Products

$y^2 - 2(y)(6) + 6^2 = y^2 - 12y + 36$

Difference of squares: $a = x$, $b = 9$

$x^2 - 81$

$(4x)^2 + 2(4x)(1) + 1^2 = 16x^2 + 8x + 1$

Solution:

Difference of squares: $a = 5x$, $b = 3$

$(5x)^2 - 3^2 = 25x^2 - 9$

Solution:

Square of a sum: $a = x$, $b = \frac{1}{2}$

$x^2 + 2(x)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = x^2 + x + \frac{1}{4}$

(a) $(x + 4)(x - 4)$

(b) $(x + 4)^2$

(c) $(x - 4)^2$

Solution:

(a) Difference of squares: $x^2 - 16$

(b) Square of a sum: $x^2 + 8x + 16$

(c) Square of a difference: $x^2 - 8x + 16$

Note how all three involve $x$ and $4$, but produce quite different results!

18. A square room has sides of length $(x + 3)$ feet. What is the area of the room in terms of $x$?

Solution:

Area of a square = (side)$^2$

Area $= (x + 3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9$ square feet

When in Doubt: Use Distribution

If you forget the special product formulas, you can always fall back on the general distributive method—it works for all polynomial multiplication. The special formulas just make common cases faster.

Example: Consider Problem 15, $(3a + 2b)(3a - 2b)$.

Using the special formula: This is a difference of squares with first term $= 3a$ and second term $= 2b$:

$(3a)^2 - (2b)^2 = 9a^2 - 4b^2$

Using distribution: Multiply every term by every term:

$3a \cdot 3a + 3a \cdot (-2b) + 2b \cdot 3a + 2b \cdot (-2b)$

$= 9a^2 - 6ab + 6ab - 4b^2$

$= 9a^2 - 4b^2$

Both methods give the same answer. The special formula is faster once you recognize the pattern, but distribution always works.

Section 14 Summary

Key Vocabulary

Term Definition
Polynomial An expression that is a sum of terms, where each term is a coefficient times variables raised to non-negative integer powers
Term A single piece of a polynomial (e.g., $3x^2$, $-5x$, $7$)
Coefficient The numerical factor in a term
Degree Of a term: the sum of exponents on variables. Of a polynomial: the highest term degree
Leading coefficient The coefficient of the highest-degree term
Standard form Terms written in descending order of degree
Monomial A polynomial with one term
Binomial A polynomial with two terms
Trinomial A polynomial with three terms

Key Skills

  • Adding polynomials: Combine like terms (same variable parts)
  • Subtracting polynomials: Distribute the minus sign to all terms being subtracted, then combine like terms
  • Multiplying polynomials: Multiply each term of the first polynomial by each term of the second, then combine like terms

Special Products

Square of a Sum:   $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$

Square of a Difference:   $(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$

Difference of Squares:   $(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$

Common Errors to Avoid

  • Forgetting to distribute the minus sign: When subtracting $(x^2 + 3x)$, change both signs: $-x^2 - 3x$
  • Missing the middle term when squaring: $(a + b)^2 \neq a^2 + b^2$. Don't forget $2ab$!
  • Confusing addition and multiplication of exponents: $x^2 + x^3 \neq x^5$ (can't combine unlike terms), but $x^2 \cdot x^3 = x^5$
  • Using FOIL for non-binomials: FOIL only works for binomial $\times$ binomial. For larger products, use general distribution.

Section 15: Factoring reverses the skills from this section. Instead of expanding $(x + 3)(x + 5)$ into $x^2 + 8x + 15$, we'll start with $x^2 + 8x + 15$ and write it as $(x + 3)(x + 5)$.

The special products become especially important:

  • Seeing $x^2 - 49$ and recognizing it as $(x + 7)(x - 7)$
  • Seeing $x^2 + 10x + 25$ and recognizing it as $(x + 5)^2$

Factoring is essential for solving quadratic equations in Section 16.

Section 12: Graphing and Linear Functions

In Section 11, we worked with inequalities on a number line—a single dimension where every point represents one number. Now we expand to two dimensions: the coordinate plane. This is where algebra becomes truly visual. Instead of just solving for unknown numbers, we can see equations as shapes—and linear equations, as we'll discover, always produce straight lines.

The coordinate plane lets us represent relationships between two quantities pictorially. If one quantity changes, how does the other respond? The graph shows us at a glance.

12.1 The Coordinate Plane

The coordinate plane is built from two number lines placed perpendicular to each other:

  • The horizontal axis is called the $x$-axis
  • The vertical axis is called the $y$-axis
  • The point where they cross is the origin, labeled $(0, 0)$

The axes divide the plane into four regions called quadrants, numbered counterclockwise starting from the upper right:

  • Quadrant I (upper right): $x$ positive, $y$ positive
  • Quadrant II (upper left): $x$ negative, $y$ positive
  • Quadrant III (lower left): $x$ negative, $y$ negative
  • Quadrant IV (lower right): $x$ positive, $y$ negative

The coordinate plane emerged independently in 1637 from two French mathematicians: René Descartes and Pierre de Fermat. Descartes published his approach in La Géométrie, an appendix to his philosophical work Discourse on the Method. Fermat's treatment circulated in manuscript form but wasn't published until after his death.

Their insight was revolutionary: geometry (shapes, positions, distances) and algebra (equations, variables, operations) could be unified. A curve could be described by an equation; an equation could be visualized as a curve. This fusion—now called analytic geometry or coordinate geometry—became one of the essential foundations for calculus, developed later that century by Newton and Leibniz.

For a fuller treatment of this pivotal development, see the Calculus Foundations appendix, Module A1.

Ordered Pairs

Every point in the plane is identified by an ordered pair $(x, y)$:

  • The first number, $x$, tells you how far to move horizontally from the origin (right if positive, left if negative)
  • The second number, $y$, tells you how far to move vertically (up if positive, down if negative)

Order Matters!

The point $(3, 5)$ is not the same as the point $(5, 3)$.

$(3, 5)$ means: go right 3, then up 5.

$(5, 3)$ means: go right 5, then up 3.

These are different locations. The $x$-coordinate always comes first.

  • Point $E = (0, 3)$ is on the $y$-axis (not in any quadrant)
  • Point $F = (-2, 0)$ is on the $x$-axis (not in any quadrant)

Points on the axes don't belong to any quadrant—they're on the boundary between quadrants.

Coordinates as an "Address System"

The perpendicular $x$-$y$ grid we've introduced is not a property of the plane itself—it's a system we impose on the plane to locate points. Think of it as an address system: the plane is like a vast open field that exists whether or not we draw lines on it. The coordinate axes are like street names we lay down so we can give directions.

The standard Cartesian system (named after Descartes) uses perpendicular axes and creates a grid of perfect squares. But this isn't the only possibility. To assign coordinates to points in a plane, we only need two pieces of information that, when combined, pinpoint exactly one location.

Alternative: Oblique Coordinates

Instead of perpendicular axes, we could use two axes that meet at any angle—say, 60°. This creates a grid of parallelograms (diamonds) instead of squares. The math works the same way; only the geometry of the grid changes. This is useful in crystallography, where atomic structures often form hexagonal patterns rather than square ones. With oblique axes tilted to match the crystal structure, atoms land on nice integer coordinates instead of messy fractions.

Oblique coordinates with axes at 60°. The point (2, 1) means "2 units along $u$, then 1 unit along $v$."

Alternative: Polar Coordinates

An even more dramatic departure: instead of "go right, then go up," we can use "face a direction, then walk a distance." In polar coordinates, a point is identified by:

  • $r$ = distance from the origin
  • $\theta$ = angle measured from the positive $x$-axis

The "grid lines" in polar coordinates are concentric circles (constant $r$) and rays emanating from the origin (constant $\theta$). This system is natural for anything involving rotation or circular symmetry—radar screens, for instance, or the orbits of planets.

Polar coordinates: each point is located by distance $r$ from origin and angle $\theta$ from the horizontal.

Why Different Systems?

The golden rule of coordinate geometry: pick the coordinate system that matches your problem. If you're analyzing a square room, Cartesian coordinates make the boundaries simple ($x = 0$, $x = 10$, etc.). If you're analyzing ripples in a circular pond, polar coordinates make the boundary simple ($r = 5$). By choosing the right "address system," complicated equations can become simple ones.

For this course, we'll work entirely with the standard perpendicular $x$-$y$ system. But knowing that alternatives exist helps you understand that coordinates are a tool we choose, not a fact about space itself.

12.2 Equations and Their Graphs

In Section 10, we solved equations like $2x + 3 = 11$, finding a single value ($x = 4$) that made the equation true. Now consider an equation with two variables:

$y = 2x + 1$

This equation doesn't have just one solution—it has infinitely many. For every value of $x$ you choose, there's a corresponding value of $y$ that makes the equation true:

  • If $x = 0$: $y = 2(0) + 1 = 1$, so $(0, 1)$ is a solution
  • If $x = 1$: $y = 2(1) + 1 = 3$, so $(1, 3)$ is a solution
  • If $x = 2$: $y = 2(2) + 1 = 5$, so $(2, 5)$ is a solution
  • If $x = -1$: $y = 2(-1) + 1 = -1$, so $(-1, -1)$ is a solution

But not every pair works. For example, $(3, 4)$ is not a solution: if $x = 3$, then $y = 2(3) + 1 = 7$, not $4$. The point $(3, 4)$ doesn't satisfy the equation.

Each solution is an ordered pair $(x, y)$—which means each solution is a point in the coordinate plane. This is the key insight: solutions to an equation become points on a graph.

Solution of an Equation in Two Variables

A solution of an equation in $x$ and $y$ is an ordered pair $(x, y)$ that makes the equation true when you substitute those values.

The graph of an equation is the set of all points $(x, y)$ that are solutions.

The Fusion of Algebra and Geometry

This definition—that the graph of an equation is the set of all its solutions—is one of the most powerful ideas in mathematics. It creates a bridge between two worlds:

  • Algebra gives us equations: symbols, operations, logical relationships
  • Geometry gives us shapes: curves, lines, surfaces we can see and draw

With this bridge, each world illuminates the other. A geometric question ("Where does this curve cross the $x$-axis?") becomes an algebraic problem (solve for $x$ when $y = 0$). An algebraic relationship ("$y$ is always twice $x$ plus one") becomes a visible shape (a line with a particular steepness).

This idea extends far beyond the flat plane. Equations in three variables ($x$, $y$, $z$) have graphs that are surfaces in three-dimensional space. The figure below shows the graph of $z = x^2 - y^2$—a "saddle surface" that curves upward in one direction and downward in another. Every point on that surface satisfies the equation; every solution to the equation lies on that surface. Testing Whether a Point Is a Solution

Given an equation and a point, you can test whether the point is a solution by substituting:

Example: Is $(3, 7)$ a solution of $y = 2x + 1$?

Substitute $x = 3$ and $y = 7$:

$$7 \stackrel{?}{=} 2(3) + 1$$

$$7 \stackrel{?}{=} 6 + 1$$

$$7 = 7 \quad \checkmark$$

Yes, $(3, 7)$ is a solution. This point lies on the graph.

Example: Is $(2, 4)$ a solution of $y = 2x + 1$?

Substitute $x = 2$ and $y = 4$:

$$4 \stackrel{?}{=} 2(2) + 1$$

$$4 \stackrel{?}{=} 4 + 1$$

$$4 \neq 5$$

No, $(2, 4)$ is not a solution. This point does not lie on the graph.

12.3 Variables: Independent and Dependent

In an equation like $y = 2x + 1$, the two variables play different roles. We choose a value for $x$, and then the equation determines the corresponding value of $y$. This asymmetry has names:

Independent and Dependent Variables

The independent variable is the one we choose freely—we control its value.

The dependent variable is the one whose value is determined by the equation once we've chosen the independent variable—it depends on our choice.

In the equation $y = 2x + 1$:

  • $x$ is the independent variable: we pick any value we like
  • $y$ is the dependent variable: its value is forced once we pick $x$

This is a convention, not an absolute rule. Mathematically, we could rewrite the same relationship as $x = \frac{y - 1}{2}$ and treat $y$ as independent. But the standard convention—$x$ independent, $y$ dependent—is so widespread that it's worth learning. When you see an equation written as "$y = \text{(something with } x\text{)}$", the author almost always intends $x$ to be the independent variable.

Variables Beyond $x$ and $y$

The letters $x$ and $y$ are conventional for abstract mathematical relationships, but real-world quantities have their own natural names:

Relationship Independent Variable Dependent Variable
Distance traveled at constant speed $t$ (time) $d$ (distance)
Cost of buying items $n$ (number of items) $C$ (total cost)
Temperature conversion $C$ (Celsius) $F$ (Fahrenheit)
Area of a square $s$ (side length) $A$ (area)
Phone bill based on data usage $g$ (gigabytes used) $C$ (monthly cost)

In each case, the independent variable is typically what we control or measure first, and the dependent variable is what results from that choice.

Graphing Convention

When graphing a relationship between two variables:

  • The independent variable goes on the horizontal axis
  • The dependent variable goes on the vertical axis

So if we're graphing distance vs. time, time ($t$) goes on the horizontal axis, and distance ($d$) goes on the vertical axis. The graph shows how distance depends on time.

Example: Identifying Variables

A car travels at a constant 60 miles per hour. The relationship between time $t$ (in hours) and distance $d$ (in miles) is:

$d = 60t$

Independent variable: $t$ (time)—we choose how long the car travels

Dependent variable: $d$ (distance)—the distance depends on how long we travel

If we graph this, $t$ goes on the horizontal axis and $d$ on the vertical axis. When $t = 2$ hours, $d = 120$ miles, giving the point $(2, 120)$.

12.4 Graphing Linear Equations

Why did all the points in Fig. 12.2 fall on a straight line? Because the equation $y = 2x - 1$ is a linear equation.

Linear Equation in Two Variables

A linear equation is an equation where the variables appear only to the first power—no $x^2$, no $y^3$, no $xy$, no $\sqrt{x}$. The defining characteristic is simply: each variable is raised only to the power of 1.

Linear equations can be written in many equivalent forms. Two common ones are:

  • Standard form: $Ax + By = C$
  • Slope-intercept form: $y = mx + b$

We'll encounter other forms (like point-slope form) later. What makes an equation "linear" isn't the form it's written in—it's the fact that $x$ and $y$ appear only to the first power.

The fundamental fact about linear equations is this: the graph of every linear equation is a straight line. This is why they're called "linear."

Why a Straight Line?

Think of a linear equation as a simple, unchanging rule. Take $y = 2x + 1$. This says: "Whatever $x$ is, double it and add 1 to get $y$."

Imagine walking along this graph, starting at any point. Each time you take one step to the right (increasing $x$ by 1), the rule forces you to go up by exactly 2 (because $y$ increases by $2 \times 1 = 2$). The instruction never changes: step right, go up 2. Step right, go up 2. Step right, go up 2.

A constant, unchanging movement rule produces a straight path. If the rule ever varied—"sometimes go up 2, sometimes go up 3"—you'd get a curve. But linear equations have constant rules, so they produce straight lines. This constant "rise per step" is exactly what we'll call slope in the next section.

Conversely, every straight line in the coordinate plane can be described by a linear equation. There's a perfect correspondence between lines (geometry) and linear equations (algebra).

How Many Points Do You Need?

Since two points determine a line, you technically need only two solutions to graph a linear equation. However, plotting a third point is good practice—it serves as an error check. If your three points don't line up, you've made a calculation mistake somewhere.

Graphing Strategy

To graph a linear equation:

  1. Find at least three solutions by choosing $x$-values and calculating $y$-values
  2. Plot these points on the coordinate plane
  3. Verify the points are collinear (lie on a line)
  4. Draw a straight line through them, extending beyond the plotted points

Intercepts

Two special points on any graph are particularly useful:

Intercepts

$x$-intercept: The point where the graph crosses the $x$-axis. At this point, $y = 0$.

$y$-intercept: The point where the graph crosses the $y$-axis. At this point, $x = 0$.

Intercepts are easy to find algebraically:

  • To find the $y$-intercept: set $x = 0$ and solve for $y$
  • To find the $x$-intercept: set $y = 0$ and solve for $x$

Example: Finding Intercepts of $y = 2x - 4$

$y$-intercept (set $x = 0$):

$$y = 2(0) - 4 = -4$$

The $y$-intercept is $(0, -4)$.

$x$-intercept (set $y = 0$):

$$0 = 2x - 4$$

$$4 = 2x$$

$$x = 2$$

The $x$-intercept is $(2, 0)$.

With these two intercepts plus one more point for verification, we can graph the line.

12.5 Slope: Measuring Steepness

Consider a ramp. Some ramps are gentle—easy to walk up. Others are steep—hard to climb. We need a way to measure this steepness precisely. In mathematics, this measurement is called slope.

Intuitively, slope answers the question: For each unit you move horizontally, how much does the line rise (or fall) vertically? Fig. 12.4 below shows several lines with different slopes—compare them visually as we develop the formal definition.

Slope

The slope of a line measures its steepness. Given two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ on a line:

$\displaystyle m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$

The letter $m$ is traditionally used for slope.

The "rise" is how much the line goes up (or down, if negative). The "run" is how much the line goes across. Slope is the ratio of these two changes—it tells you how much you rise or fall for each step forward.

Example: Calculating Slope from Two Points

Find the slope of the line through $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 8)$.

Using the slope formula with $(x_1, y_1) = (1, 2)$ and $(x_2, y_2) = (4, 8)$:

$$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{8 - 2}{4 - 1} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$

The slope is $2$. This means: for every 1 unit you move to the right, the line rises 2 units.

Example: Slope with a Negative Result

Find the slope of the line through $(0, 5)$ and $(3, 2)$.

$$m = \frac{2 - 5}{3 - 0} = \frac{-3}{3} = -1$$

The slope is $-1$. The negative sign tells us the line goes down as we move right. For every 1 unit to the right, the line falls 1 unit.

Interpreting Slope Values

The sign and magnitude of slope tell us about the line's direction and steepness:

Slope Direction Description
$m > 0$ (positive) Rises left to right Line goes uphill
$m < 0$ (negative) Falls left to right Line goes downhill
$m = 0$ Horizontal Flat—no rise at all
$m$ undefined Vertical No run—division by zero

The larger the absolute value of the slope, the steeper the line. A slope of $3$ is steeper than a slope of $\frac{1}{2}$. A slope of $-4$ is steeper than a slope of $-1$ (both go downhill, but $-4$ is steeper).

Horizontal and Vertical Lines

Horizontal lines have equation $y = c$ (where $c$ is a constant). The slope is $0$ because there's no rise—the line is flat.

Vertical lines have equation $x = c$. The slope is undefined because the run is $0$, and we can't divide by zero.

Fig. 12.4: Comparing Slopes
Lines with different slopes: steep positive ($m = 2$), gentle positive ($m = \frac{1}{2}$), negative ($m = -1$), and horizontal ($m = 0$). The vertical line $x = 4$ has undefined slope.

Slope as Rate of Change

Slope isn't just about steepness on a graph—it measures how quickly one quantity changes relative to another. This interpretation becomes crucial in applications. When we have real-world variables, slope tells us the rate: how much the dependent variable changes for each unit change in the independent variable.

Example: Slope as Speed

A car's distance from home is recorded over time. Let $t$ represent time (in hours) and $d$ represent distance (in miles). The data points $(1, 50)$ and $(3, 150)$ lie on the graph—meaning at $t = 1$ hour the car was 50 miles away, and at $t = 3$ hours it was 150 miles away.

Here $t$ is the independent variable (horizontal axis) and $d$ is the dependent variable (vertical axis). The slope formula becomes:

$$m = \frac{d_2 - d_1}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{150 - 50}{3 - 1} = \frac{100 \text{ miles}}{2 \text{ hours}} = 50 \text{ miles per hour}$$

The slope is 50—the car's speed. Slope = rate of change of distance with respect to time = speed.

Example: Slope as Cost Rate

A phone plan's monthly cost depends on data usage. Let $g$ represent data used (in gigabytes) and $C$ represent the monthly cost (in dollars). The graph passes through $(2, 45)$ and $(5, 75)$—meaning 2 GB costs 45 dollars and 5 GB costs 75 dollars.

Here $g$ is the independent variable and $C$ is the dependent variable. Applying the slope formula:

$$m = \frac{C_2 - C_1}{g_2 - g_1} = \frac{75 - 45}{5 - 2} = \frac{30 \text{ dollars}}{3 \text{ GB}} = 10 \text{ dollars per GB}$$

The slope is 10—the plan charges 10 dollars per gigabyte of data used.

In these examples, slope has real-world meaning: speed, cost per unit, rate of growth. Whenever you see a linear relationship, the slope tells you how fast the dependent variable changes as the independent variable changes.

12.6 Slope-Intercept Form

In Section 12.5, we defined slope as $m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$—the ratio of vertical change to horizontal change, often called "rise over run." Now we'll see how slope and the $y$-intercept come together in the most useful form of a linear equation.

Slope-Intercept Form

A linear equation written as

$y = mx + b$

is in slope-intercept form, where:

  • $m$ is the slope of the line
  • $b$ is the $y$-intercept (the $y$-coordinate where the line crosses the $y$-axis)

This isn't just a convenient formula to memorize—there are clear reasons why $m$ represents slope and $b$ represents the $y$-intercept. Understanding these reasons makes the formula meaningful rather than arbitrary.

Why $b$ Is the $y$-Intercept

The $y$-intercept is where the line crosses the $y$-axis. On the $y$-axis, the $x$-coordinate is always $0$. So to find the $y$-intercept, we substitute $x = 0$ into the equation:

$$y = m(0) + b = 0 + b = b$$

When $x = 0$, $y = b$. The line passes through the point $(0, b)$—which is exactly what "$y$-intercept equals $b$" means.

Fig. 12.5a: Why $b$ Is the $y$-Intercept
Three lines with the same slope ($m = 1$) but different $y$-intercepts. Each line crosses the $y$-axis at its $b$ value: $y = x + 3$ crosses at $(0, 3)$, $y = x$ crosses at $(0, 0)$, and $y = x - 2$ crosses at $(0, -2)$.

Why $m$ Is the Slope

Let's verify that the coefficient $m$ really is the slope by using the slope formula from Section 12.5. Take any two points on the line $y = mx + b$:

  • At $x = x_1$, we get $y_1 = mx_1 + b$
  • At $x = x_2$, we get $y_2 = mx_2 + b$

Now apply the slope formula:

$$\text{slope} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{(mx_2 + b) - (mx_1 + b)}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{mx_2 - mx_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{m(x_2 - x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = m$$

No matter which two points you choose, the slope formula gives $m$. The coefficient of $x$ in $y = mx + b$ is the slope.

This also tells us how to interpret $m$ geometrically. Since $m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$:

  • If $m = 2 = \frac{2}{1}$, then $\Delta y = 2$ when $\Delta x = 1$: the line rises 2 units for every 1 unit to the right
  • If $m = \frac{2}{3}$, then $\Delta y = 2$ when $\Delta x = 3$: the line rises 2 units for every 3 units to the right
  • If $m = -\frac{1}{2}$, then $\Delta y = -1$ when $\Delta x = 2$: the line falls 1 unit for every 2 units to the right

Four lines through the same $y$-intercept $(0, 2)$ but with different slopes. Steeper lines have larger $|m|$. Positive $m$ rises left-to-right; negative $m$ falls.

  2. Use the slope to find another point: From $(0, b)$, use $m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ to move to a second point. If $m = \frac{2}{3}$, move right 3 units ($\Delta x = 3$) and up 2 units ($\Delta y = 2$). If $m = -\frac{1}{4}$, move right 4 units and down 1 unit.

12.7 Other Forms of Linear Equations

Slope-intercept form is convenient for graphing and for reading off the slope and intercept. But linear equations appear in other forms too, each useful in different situations.

Standard Form

Standard Form

A linear equation in standard form is written as:

$Ax + By = C$

where $A$, $B$, and $C$ are constants, and typically $A \geq 0$.

Standard form is common in applications (especially systems of equations, which we'll study in Section 13) and makes finding intercepts easy:

  • $x$-intercept: Set $y = 0$ and solve for $x$
  • $y$-intercept: Set $x = 0$ and solve for $y$

Example: Finding Intercepts from Standard Form

Find the intercepts of $3x + 2y = 12$.

$x$-intercept (set $y = 0$):

$3x + 2(0) = 12$

$3x = 12$

$x = 4$

The $x$-intercept is $(4, 0)$.

$y$-intercept (set $x = 0$):

$3(0) + 2y = 12$

$2y = 12$

$y = 6$

The $y$-intercept is $(0, 6)$.

Converting Standard Form to Slope-Intercept Form

To convert from standard form to slope-intercept form, solve for $y$:

Example: Convert $2x + 3y = 12$ to Slope-Intercept Form

Start with: $2x + 3y = 12$

Subtract $2x$ from both sides:

$3y = -2x + 12$

Divide both sides by $3$:

$y = -\frac{2}{3}x + 4$

Now we can read off: slope $m = -\frac{2}{3}$, $y$-intercept $b = 4$.

Point-Slope Form

What if you know a point on a line and its slope, but the point isn't the $y$-intercept? We can derive a useful formula directly from the slope definition.

Suppose a line has slope $m$ and passes through the known point $(x_1, y_1)$. Let $(x, y)$ be any other point on the line. Since both points lie on the same line, the slope between them must equal $m$:

$$m = \frac{y - y_1}{x - x_1}$$

Multiply both sides by $(x - x_1)$:

$$m(x - x_1) = y - y_1$$

Rewriting with $y - y_1$ on the left:

Point-Slope Form

If a line has slope $m$ and passes through the point $(x_1, y_1)$, its equation can be written as:

$y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$

This formula comes directly from the definition of slope: it says that the slope between any point $(x, y)$ on the line and the known point $(x_1, y_1)$ equals $m$.

Point-slope form is useful when you know:

  • A point on the line, AND
  • The slope of the line

Example: Write the Equation Using Point-Slope Form

Write the equation of the line through $(2, 5)$ with slope $3$.

Solution: Use point-slope form with $(x_1, y_1) = (2, 5)$ and $m = 3$:

$$y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$$

$$y - 5 = 3(x - 2)$$

This is the equation in point-slope form. We can also convert to slope-intercept form:

$$y - 5 = 3x - 6$$

$$y = 3x - 1$$

Watch the Signs!

The point-slope formula has subtraction: $y - y_1$ and $x - x_1$.

If your point has negative coordinates, be careful:

For the point $(-3, 4)$: $\quad y - 4 = m(x - (-3)) = m(x + 3)$

For the point $(2, -5)$: $\quad y - (-5) = m(x - 2)$, which simplifies to $y + 5 = m(x - 2)$

Sign errors here are common—double-check your substitution.

Finding an Equation from Two Points

What if you're given two points but no slope? You can find the equation in two steps:

Two-Point Method

  1. Calculate the slope from the two points: $m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$
  2. Use point-slope form with the slope you found and either point

Example: Find the Equation Through Two Points

Write the equation of the line through $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 8)$.

Step 1: Find the slope.

$$m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{8 - 2}{4 - 1} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$

Step 2: Use point-slope form with either point.

Using $(1, 2)$:

$$y - 2 = 2(x - 1)$$

Step 3: Simplify to slope-intercept form (optional).

$$y - 2 = 2x - 2$$

$$y = 2x$$

Check: Does $(4, 8)$ satisfy $y = 2x$? Yes: $8 = 2(4)$. ✓

Any Form Gets You There

Students sometimes worry about using the "wrong" form. Here's an important principle: any form will give you the correct equation—some paths are just shorter than others.

Let's demonstrate by solving the same problem two different ways:

Example: Same Problem, Two Approaches

Problem: Find the equation of the line through $(2, 7)$ with slope $3$.

Approach 1: Point-Slope Form (natural choice)

We have a point and a slope—point-slope form is made for this:

$$y - 7 = 3(x - 2)$$

$$y - 7 = 3x - 6$$

$$y = 3x + 1$$

Done in 3 steps.

Approach 2: Slope-Intercept Form (longer path)

We could insist on using $y = mx + b$ directly. We know $m = 3$, so:

$$y = 3x + b$$

Now we need to find $b$. Since $(2, 7)$ is on the line, substitute $x = 2$ and $y = 7$:

$$7 = 3(2) + b$$

$$7 = 6 + b$$

$$b = 1$$

So $y = 3x + 1$.

Done in 5 steps—same answer, more work.

Example: Another Comparison

Problem: Find the equation of the line through $(-1, 4)$ and $(3, -2)$.

Approach 1: Two-Point Method (natural choice)

Step 1—Find slope: $m = \frac{-2 - 4}{3 - (-1)} = \frac{-6}{4} = -\frac{3}{2}$

Step 2—Point-slope with $(-1, 4)$:

$$y - 4 = -\frac{3}{2}(x - (-1))$$

$$y - 4 = -\frac{3}{2}(x + 1)$$

$$y - 4 = -\frac{3}{2}x - \frac{3}{2}$$

$$y = -\frac{3}{2}x + \frac{5}{2}$$

Approach 2: Slope-Intercept Form

Step 1—Find slope (same as above): $m = -\frac{3}{2}$

Step 2—Write $y = -\frac{3}{2}x + b$ and substitute one point to find $b$.

Using $(-1, 4)$:

$$4 = -\frac{3}{2}(-1) + b$$

$$4 = \frac{3}{2} + b$$

$$b = 4 - \frac{3}{2} = \frac{8}{2} - \frac{3}{2} = \frac{5}{2}$$

So $y = -\frac{3}{2}x + \frac{5}{2}$.

Same answer—both approaches work!

The Takeaway

You can solve any linear equation problem using any form. Choosing the "right" form makes the work easier, but choosing the "wrong" form doesn't give you the wrong answer—it just gives you more work. If you get stuck or confused, you can always:

  1. Find the slope (using the slope formula if needed)
  2. Find $b$ by substituting a known point into $y = mx + b$

This "brute force" method always works.

12.8 Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

Two special relationships between lines arise constantly in geometry and applications: lines that never meet (parallel) and lines that meet at right angles (perpendicular). Both relationships have simple characterizations in terms of slope. (For geometric derivations of these relationships, see the "Going Deeper" box at the end of this section.)

Parallel Lines

Parallel Lines

Two lines are parallel if they never intersect—they maintain the same distance apart everywhere.

Algebraically: Parallel lines have the same slope.

If line 1 has slope $m_1$ and line 2 has slope $m_2$, the lines are parallel when $m_1 = m_2$.

This makes intuitive sense: if two lines rise at exactly the same rate (same $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$), they're tilted the same way and will never cross. They're like two railroad tracks—always the same distance apart. (For a more rigorous argument, see the "Going Deeper" box below.)

Example: Identifying Parallel Lines

Are the lines $y = 3x + 5$ and $y = 3x - 2$ parallel?

Solution: Both lines have slope $m = 3$. Same slope → parallel lines. ✓

Note: They have different $y$-intercepts ($5$ and $-2$), so they're not the same line—they're distinct parallel lines.

Example: Finding a Parallel Line

Find the equation of the line parallel to $y = 2x + 3$ that passes through $(1, 7)$.

Solution:

Step 1: The given line has slope $m = 2$. A parallel line must also have slope $2$.

Step 2: Use point-slope form with $m = 2$ and the point $(1, 7)$:

$$y - 7 = 2(x - 1)$$

$$y - 7 = 2x - 2$$

$$y = 2x + 5$$

This line has the same slope as $y = 2x + 3$ but a different $y$-intercept, so it's parallel.

Perpendicular Lines

Perpendicular Lines

Two lines are perpendicular if they intersect at a right angle (90°).

Algebraically: Perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals.

If line 1 has slope $m_1$ and line 2 has slope $m_2$, the lines are perpendicular when $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$.

Equivalently: $m_2 = -\frac{1}{m_1}$

The "negative reciprocal" means: flip the fraction and change the sign. Why does this work? See the "Going Deeper" box below for a geometric explanation.

If $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$, the lines are perpendicular.

Special Cases: Horizontal and Vertical Lines

The slope formula helps us understand horizontal and vertical lines:

Horizontal lines ($y = c$): Any two points on a horizontal line have the same $y$-coordinate, so $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1 = 0$. Therefore:

$$m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{0}{\Delta x} = 0$$

Horizontal lines have slope $0$. They are all parallel to each other (all have the same slope).

Vertical lines ($x = c$): Any two points on a vertical line have the same $x$-coordinate, so $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1 = 0$. Therefore:

$$m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta y}{0} = \text{undefined}$$

Vertical lines have undefined slope (division by zero). They are all parallel to each other.

Horizontal ⊥ Vertical: The "negative reciprocal" rule doesn't apply directly here (you can't take the reciprocal of undefined or $0$), but geometrically it's clear: horizontal and vertical lines meet at right angles, so they are perpendicular.

Why These Slope Rules Work

We've stated that parallel lines have equal slopes and perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is $-1$. But why are these statements true? Let's look deeper and see the reasoning from both geometry and algebra. It's not essential that you master every detail here, but it gives you a flavor for mathematical reasoning—and demonstrates how the same question can be approached from different angles.

Parallel Lines: The Geometric Argument

Think about what slope means geometrically: $m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ measures how much the line rises per unit of horizontal distance. If two lines have the same slope, they rise at the same rate—they're tilted at exactly the same angle to the horizontal.

Two lines tilted at the same angle either coincide (are the same line) or never meet. To see why they never meet if they're distinct: suppose they did meet at some point $P$. At that point, both lines extend in exactly the same direction (same slope = same tilt). But then they would have to be the same line! Since they're not the same line (different $y$-intercepts), they can't meet anywhere. That's the definition of parallel.

Parallel Lines: The Algebraic Argument

The geometric argument is intuitively clear, but perhaps a bit "hand-wavy." Part of the power of what we're learning is that you can examine the same question from either a geometric or an algebraic viewpoint. Let's use algebra to prove that lines with different slopes must meet, while lines with the same slope (but different intercepts) cannot.

Consider two lines: $y = m_1 x + b_1$ and $y = m_2 x + b_2$. If the lines meet at a point $(p, q)$, then that point lies on both lines, so:

$$q = m_1 p + b_1 \quad \text{and} \quad q = m_2 p + b_2$$

Since both expressions equal $q$, we can set them equal:

$$m_1 p + b_1 = m_2 p + b_2$$

Rearranging to solve for $p$:

$$(m_1 - m_2)p = b_2 - b_1$$

$$p = \frac{b_2 - b_1}{m_1 - m_2}$$

Case 1: Different slopes ($m_1 \neq m_2$). The denominator is nonzero, so we can compute $p$, and the lines intersect. A concrete example: suppose the lines are $y = 2x - 4$ and $y = 4x + 1$. The $x$-coordinate of intersection is:

$$p = \frac{1 - (-4)}{2 - 4} = \frac{5}{-2} = -\frac{5}{2}$$

To find $q$, substitute into either equation. Using the first: $q = 2(-\frac{5}{2}) - 4 = -5 - 4 = -9$. Check with the second: $q = 4(-\frac{5}{2}) + 1 = -10 + 1 = -9$. ✓ The lines meet at $(-\frac{5}{2}, -9)$.

Case 2: Same slope ($m_1 = m_2$). Now $m_1 - m_2 = 0$, and computing $p = \frac{b_2 - b_1}{0}$ requires division by zero—there is no such $p$. Going back to our equations $m_1 p + b_1 = m_2 p + b_2$, if the slopes are equal, this simplifies to $b_1 = b_2$. But we assumed the lines are distinct (different intercepts), so $b_1 \neq b_2$. Contradiction! The lines cannot meet.

This is a geometric conclusion—"distinct lines with equal slopes don't intersect"—proved entirely by algebra.

Perpendicular Lines: A Geometric Argument

The algebraic proof for perpendicular lines requires tools beyond this course, but we can give a convincing geometric argument. Consider a line with slope $m = \frac{a}{b}$, where $a$ is the rise and $b$ is the run. Now imagine rotating this line 90° counterclockwise.

Under a 90° counterclockwise rotation:

  • A horizontal movement (run) of $b$ units to the right becomes a vertical movement of $b$ units upward
  • A vertical movement (rise) of $a$ units upward becomes a horizontal movement of $a$ units to the left

So the original "rise over run" of $\frac{a}{b}$ becomes a new rise of $b$ and a new run of $-a$ (negative because leftward). The new slope is:

$$m_{\text{rotated}} = \frac{b}{-a} = -\frac{b}{a} = -\frac{1}{m}$$

The slope of the rotated (perpendicular) line is the negative reciprocal of the original slope. Multiplying them:

$$m \cdot m_{\text{rotated}} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \left(-\frac{b}{a}\right) = -1$$

This is why perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is $-1$: rotating a line 90° transforms its slope to its negative reciprocal.

12.9 Applications

Linear equations appear everywhere in the real world. In this section, we'll see how to build and interpret linear models—using slope and intercept to understand practical situations.

Mathematical Modeling

To use mathematics for real-world problems, you must make assumptions to create a mathematical model—a simplified representation of reality that can be analyzed with mathematical tools.

The quality of your answers depends not just on how well you do the math, but on how well your model matches the real situation. We often simplify a problem to get equations we can solve. A good scientist or engineer creates models that are complex enough to capture the essential features, yet simple enough to be tractable.

In this section, we focus on situations where linear models provide useful first approximations. In later lessons, we'll see how to create more sophisticated models and use more advanced mathematics when greater accuracy is needed.

Slope as Rate of Change

We've seen that slope measures how much $y$ changes per unit change in $x$. In applications, this becomes a rate: dollars per hour, miles per gallon, degrees per minute, and so on.

Interpreting Slope in Context

When a linear equation models a real situation:

  • The slope tells you the rate of change—how fast the output changes per unit of input
  • The units of slope are (units of $y$) per (unit of $x$)

For example, if $y$ is distance in miles and $x$ is time in hours, then the slope has units of miles per hour—it's a speed.

The $y$-Intercept in Context

The $y$-intercept is the value of $y$ when $x = 0$. In applications, this often represents a starting value or fixed amount:

  • A taxi charges a base fare plus a rate per mile. The base fare is the $y$-intercept (cost when miles = 0).
  • A phone costs a certain amount plus a monthly fee. The purchase price is the $y$-intercept.
  • A candle burns down at a steady rate. The initial height is the $y$-intercept (height at time = 0).

Example: Cost Function

A rideshare service charges a 2.50 dollar base fare plus 1.75 dollars per mile. Let $C$ be the total cost (in dollars) and $m$ be the distance traveled (in miles).

(a) Write a linear equation relating $C$ and $m$.

The cost has two parts: a fixed base fare (2.50 dollars) plus a variable amount that depends on miles (1.75 dollars per mile). This gives:

$$C = 1.75m + 2.50$$

(b) Identify the slope and $y$-intercept. What do they mean?

Slope: $1.75$ dollars per mile. This is the rate—each additional mile costs 1.75 dollars.

$y$-intercept: $2.50$ dollars. This is the base fare—the cost of a ride even before traveling any distance.

(c) How much does a 12-mile ride cost?

$$C = 1.75(12) + 2.50 = 21.00 + 2.50 = 23.50 \text{ dollars}$$

(d) If you have 30 dollars, how far can you travel?

Set $C = 30$ and solve for $m$:

$$30 = 1.75m + 2.50$$

$$27.50 = 1.75m$$

$$m = \frac{27.50}{1.75} \approx 15.7 \text{ miles}$$

Rideshare Cost vs. Distance
The cost function $C = 1.75m + 2.50$. The $y$-intercept (2.50) is the base fare; the slope (1.75) is the cost per mile.

Example: Temperature Conversion

The relationship between Celsius ($C$) and Fahrenheit ($F$) temperatures is:

$$F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32$$

(a) Identify the slope and $y$-intercept. What do they mean?

Slope: $\frac{9}{5} = 1.8$ degrees Fahrenheit per degree Celsius. For each 1°C increase, the Fahrenheit temperature increases by 1.8°F. (Fahrenheit degrees are "smaller" than Celsius degrees.)

$y$-intercept: $32$°F. When $C = 0$ (the freezing point of water in Celsius), $F = 32$ (the freezing point in Fahrenheit).

(b) Convert 25°C to Fahrenheit.

$$F = \frac{9}{5}(25) + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77°\text{F}$$

(c) At what temperature are Celsius and Fahrenheit equal?

Set $F = C$ and solve:

$$C = \frac{9}{5}C + 32$$

$$C - \frac{9}{5}C = 32$$

$$-\frac{4}{5}C = 32$$

$$C = 32 \times \left(-\frac{5}{4}\right) = -40$$

At $-40$ degrees, Celsius and Fahrenheit readings are the same: $-40°\text{C} = -40°\text{F}$.

Celsius to Fahrenheit Conversion
The conversion formula $F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32$. The $y$-intercept (32) is the freezing point of water in Fahrenheit. The lines $F = C$ and $F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32$ intersect at $(-40, -40)$.

Example: Interpreting a Graph

The graph below shows the amount of water in a tank over time as it drains.

Fig. 12.7: Water Draining from a Tank
Water volume $V$ (gallons) versus time $t$ (minutes). The tank starts full and drains at a constant rate.

(a) What is the initial amount of water?

The $y$-intercept is at $(0, 120)$, so the tank initially contains 120 gallons.

(b) What is the rate of drainage?

The line passes through $(0, 120)$ and $(30, 0)$. The slope is:

$$m = \frac{0 - 120}{30 - 0} = \frac{-120}{30} = -4 \text{ gallons per minute}$$

The negative slope indicates decreasing volume. The tank loses 4 gallons per minute.

(c) Write the equation and predict when the tank will be half empty.

The equation is $V = -4t + 120$.

Half of 120 gallons is 60 gallons. Set $V = 60$:

$$60 = -4t + 120$$

$$-60 = -4t$$

$$t = 15 \text{ minutes}$$

(d) When will the tank be empty?

Set $V = 0$:

$$0 = -4t + 120$$

$$t = 30 \text{ minutes}$$

This matches the $x$-intercept of the graph.

A note on modeling: This example assumes a constant drainage rate, which is an idealization. If the tank drains through a pump running at fixed speed, a constant rate is realistic. But if water flows out through a spigot at the bottom, the rate depends on water pressure—which decreases as the tank empties. In that case, drainage would be fast at first and slow near the end, and our linear model would underestimate the total time to empty.

Our linear model gives a useful first approximation, but a more accurate prediction for gravity-fed drainage would require a nonlinear model (which we'll encounter in later courses). Recognizing when a linear approximation is "good enough" versus when you need more sophisticated mathematics is an important skill in applied work.

Building Linear Models

To model a situation with a linear equation:

  1. Identify the variables: What quantities are changing? Which is independent ($x$) and which is dependent ($y$)?
  2. Find the slope: What is the rate of change? (Look for phrases like "per unit," "each," "for every.")
  3. Find the $y$-intercept: What is the starting value when $x = 0$? (Look for fixed costs, initial amounts, base values.)
  4. Write the equation: $y = mx + b$
  5. Check units: Make sure the slope has the right units and the answer makes sense.

Section 12 Summary

Key Concepts

The Coordinate Plane

  • The coordinate plane is formed by two perpendicular number lines: the horizontal $x$-axis and vertical $y$-axis, meeting at the origin $(0, 0)$.
  • Every point is identified by an ordered pair $(x, y)$.
  • The plane is divided into four quadrants, numbered I–IV counterclockwise from the upper right.

Linear Equations and Their Graphs

  • A linear equation in two variables (like $y = 2x + 3$ or $3x - y = 7$) graphs as a straight line.
  • Every point on the line is a solution of the equation; every solution is a point on the line.
  • To graph a line, find at least two points that satisfy the equation.

Slope

  • Slope measures steepness: $m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$ ("rise over run").
  • Positive slope: line rises left to right. Negative slope: line falls left to right.
  • Zero slope: horizontal line. Undefined slope: vertical line.
  • In applications, slope represents a rate of change (dollars per hour, miles per gallon, etc.).

Forms of Linear Equations

  • Slope-intercept form: $y = mx + b$, where $m$ is slope and $b$ is the $y$-intercept.
  • Point-slope form: $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$, useful when you know a point and the slope.
  • Standard form: $Ax + By = C$, useful for finding intercepts and solving systems.
  • Any form can be converted to any other form. The choice affects convenience, not correctness.

Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

  • Parallel lines have the same slope (and different $y$-intercepts).
  • Perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals: $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$.
  • Horizontal and vertical lines are perpendicular to each other.

Interpreting Linear Models

  • The slope tells you the rate of change—how much the output changes per unit of input.
  • The $y$-intercept tells you the starting value—the output when the input is zero.

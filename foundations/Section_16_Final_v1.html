Section 16: Quadratic Equations

In Section 15, we learned to factor polynomials. At the end, we glimpsed why this matters: if $x^2 - 5x + 6$ factors as $(x - 2)(x - 3)$, then the equation $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$ has solutions $x = 2$ and $x = 3$. The factored form reveals the solutions directly.

Now we develop this connection systematically. Quadratic equations—equations where the highest power of the variable is 2—appear throughout science, engineering, and everyday problem-solving. They describe the path of a thrown ball, the shape of satellite dishes, and the behavior of electrical circuits. Learning to solve them opens doors to understanding how the physical world works.

16.1 What is a Quadratic Equation?

In Section 10, we solved linear equations—equations where the variable appears only to the first power, like $3x + 5 = 14$. A quadratic equation takes one step higher: the variable can appear squared.

Quadratic Equation

A quadratic equation in one variable is an equation that can be written in the form

$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants and $a \neq 0$. This is called standard form.

The requirement $a \neq 0$ is essential: if $a = 0$, the $x^2$ term vanishes and you're left with a linear equation.

The coefficients have names: $a$ is the leading coefficient (it leads the highest-power term), $b$ is the linear coefficient, and $c$ is the constant term.

Recognizing Quadratic Equations

A quadratic equation has an $x^2$ term as its highest power. Here are some examples:

Quadratic equations:

  • $x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0$ — already in standard form with $a = 1$, $b = 5$, $c = 6$
  • $3x^2 - 7 = 0$ — standard form with $a = 3$, $b = 0$, $c = -7$ (the linear term is missing)
  • $2x^2 = 8x - 3$ — quadratic, but needs rearrangement to reach standard form
  • $x^2 = 16$ — quadratic with $b = 0$ and $c = -16$ when written as $x^2 - 16 = 0$

Not quadratic equations:

  • $x^3 + 2x = 5$ — this is cubic (highest power is 3)
  • $2x + 3 = 0$ — this is linear (highest power is 1)
  • $\frac{1}{x} + x = 2$ — the term $\frac{1}{x}$ makes this a rational equation, not a polynomial

Example 1: Identifying Standard Form Coefficients

For each quadratic equation, write it in standard form and identify $a$, $b$, and $c$.

(a) $x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0$

Already in standard form. Reading off the coefficients: $a = 1$, $b = -4$, $c = 3$.

(b) $5x^2 + 2x = 0$

Already in standard form (the constant term is zero). We have $a = 5$, $b = 2$, $c = 0$.

(c) $3x^2 = 12$

Subtract 12 from both sides: $3x^2 - 12 = 0$. Now it's in standard form with $a = 3$, $b = 0$, $c = -12$.

(d) $x = 2x^2 - 5$

Move all terms to one side: $0 = 2x^2 - x - 5$, or equivalently $2x^2 - x - 5 = 0$. This gives $a = 2$, $b = -1$, $c = -5$.

(e) $7 - x^2 = 4x$

Rearrange: $-x^2 - 4x + 7 = 0$. This is valid standard form with $a = -1$, $b = -4$, $c = 7$.

Alternatively, multiply through by $-1$ to get $x^2 + 4x - 7 = 0$, giving $a = 1$, $b = 4$, $c = -7$. Both forms are correct; the second is often preferred because $a > 0$.

Why Standard Form Matters

Converting to standard form isn't just bureaucratic tidiness. It serves two purposes:

  • It reveals structure. Once in standard form, you can immediately see the coefficients that determine the equation's behavior.
  • It enables systematic methods. The quadratic formula (Section 16.5) uses $a$, $b$, and $c$ directly. You can't apply it until you've identified these values.

Quadratic equations have been studied for nearly 4,000 years. Babylonian scribes around 1800 BCE solved problems equivalent to our quadratics using geometric cut-and-paste methods on clay tablets. They could find the side of a square given its area, or solve problems like "a field's length exceeds its width by 7, and its area is 60"—which we'd write as $x(x + 7) = 60$, or $x^2 + 7x - 60 = 0$.

The algebraic approach we use today was systematized by the Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi in his 9th-century treatise Al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr wal-Muqabala ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing"). The word al-jabr in the title gave us "algebra." Al-Khwarizmi classified quadratic equations into types and gave systematic recipes for solving each. We'll see more of this history when we derive the quadratic formula in Section 16.5.

16.2 The Zero Product Property

In Section 15, we learned to factor expressions like $x^2 + 5x + 6$ into $(x + 2)(x + 3)$. To turn this skill into a solving method, we need one key principle.

Zero Product Property

If $ab = 0$, then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$ (or both).

In words: if a product of factors equals zero, at least one of the factors must be zero.

This property might seem obvious, but it's worth pausing to see why it's true and why it's special to zero.

Why it works: Suppose $ab = 0$ but $a \neq 0$. Since $a$ isn't zero, we can divide both sides by $a$: we get $b = 0/a = 0$. So if $a$ isn't zero, then $b$ must be. Similarly, if $b$ isn't zero, then $a$ must be. At least one of them has to be zero for their product to be zero.

Why zero is special: This property doesn't work for any other number. If $ab = 6$, you can't conclude that $a = 6$ or $b = 6$. (Consider $a = 2$, $b = 3$.) But zero has a unique "absorbing" property in multiplication: anything times zero is zero. This makes it the only number where the product being zero forces one of the factors to be zero.

The Bridge from Factoring to Solving

The Zero Product Property is what makes factoring useful for solving equations. If we can write an equation as

(something)(something else) $= 0$

then we know at least one of those "somethings" must equal zero. This transforms one complicated equation into two simpler ones.

Example 2: Direct Application

Solve $(x - 5)(x + 1) = 0$.

Solution:

The equation is already factored. By the Zero Product Property, either the first factor is zero or the second factor is zero:

$x - 5 = 0$   or   $x + 1 = 0$

Solving each linear equation:

$x = 5$   or   $x = -1$

Answer: $x = 5$ or $x = -1$

Check: Substitute $x = 5$: $(5 - 5)(5 + 1) = (0)(6) = 0$ ✓
Substitute $x = -1$: $(-1 - 5)(-1 + 1) = (-6)(0) = 0$ ✓

Example 3: Solutions Including Fractions

Solve $(2x + 3)(x - 4) = 0$.

Solution:

By the Zero Product Property:

$2x + 3 = 0$   or   $x - 4 = 0$

From the first equation: $2x = -3$, so $x = -\frac{3}{2}$.

From the second equation: $x = 4$.

Answer: $x = -\frac{3}{2}$ or $x = 4$

The Property Only Works When One Side Is Zero

Consider the equation $(x + 2)(x + 3) = 12$. You might be tempted to conclude that $x + 2 = 12$ or $x + 3 = 12$, giving $x = 10$ or $x = 9$.

This is wrong. Check: $(10 + 2)(10 + 3) = 12 \times 13 = 156 \neq 12$.

The Zero Product Property applies only when the product equals zero. For other values, you must expand, rearrange to standard form, and then factor.

Correct approach: Expand $(x + 2)(x + 3) = x^2 + 5x + 6$. Then $x^2 + 5x + 6 = 12$ becomes $x^2 + 5x - 6 = 0$. Factor: $(x + 6)(x - 1) = 0$. Solutions: $x = -6$ or $x = 1$.

16.3 Solving Quadratic Equations by Factoring

Now we combine our factoring skills from Section 15 with the Zero Product Property to develop a systematic solving method.

Method: Solving by Factoring

  1. Write the equation in standard form: $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$
  2. Factor the left side completely.
  3. Apply the Zero Product Property: Set each factor equal to zero.
  4. Solve each resulting linear equation.
  5. Check your solutions in the original equation (optional but recommended).

Example 4: A Simple Trinomial

Solve $x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0$.

Solution:

Step 1: The equation is already in standard form.

Step 2: Factor the left side. We need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5. Those are 2 and 3.

$x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)$

Step 3: Apply the Zero Product Property.

$(x + 2)(x + 3) = 0$ means $x + 2 = 0$ or $x + 3 = 0$

Step 4: Solve each equation.

$x = -2$ or $x = -3$

Step 5: Check.

For $x = -2$: $(-2)^2 + 5(-2) + 6 = 4 - 10 + 6 = 0$ ✓

For $x = -3$: $(-3)^2 + 5(-3) + 6 = 9 - 15 + 6 = 0$ ✓

Answer: $x = -2$ or $x = -3$

Example 5: Difference of Squares

Solve $x^2 - 9 = 0$.

Solution:

This is a difference of squares: $x^2 - 9 = x^2 - 3^2 = (x + 3)(x - 3)$.

Setting each factor to zero:

$x + 3 = 0$ gives $x = -3$

$x - 3 = 0$ gives $x = 3$

Answer: $x = -3$ or $x = 3$ (often written as $x = \pm 3$)

Alternative approach: You could also add 9 to both sides to get $x^2 = 9$, then take the square root: $x = \pm\sqrt{9} = \pm 3$. Both methods work.

Example 6: Rearrangement Required

Solve $x^2 = 5x$.

Solution:

First, write in standard form by subtracting $5x$ from both sides:

$x^2 - 5x = 0$

Factor out the GCF, which is $x$:

$x(x - 5) = 0$

Apply the Zero Product Property:

$x = 0$ or $x - 5 = 0$

$x = 0$ or $x = 5$

Answer: $x = 0$ or $x = 5$

Never Divide Both Sides by the Variable

In Example 6, you might be tempted to "simplify" by dividing both sides of $x^2 = 5x$ by $x$:

$\frac{x^2}{x} = \frac{5x}{x}$   →   $x = 5$

This loses the solution $x = 0$.

When you divide by $x$, you're implicitly assuming $x \neq 0$. But $x = 0$ is a valid solution—check: $0^2 = 5(0)$ gives $0 = 0$ ✓.

The safe approach: Always move all terms to one side and factor. Never divide by an expression containing the variable unless you're certain that expression cannot be zero.

Example 7: GCF with a Coefficient

Solve $3x^2 - 12x = 0$.

Solution:

Factor out the GCF. Both terms share a factor of $3x$:

$3x^2 - 12x = 3x(x - 4) = 0$

Apply the Zero Product Property. Note that $3x = 0$ means $x = 0$:

$3x = 0$ gives $x = 0$

$x - 4 = 0$ gives $x = 4$

Answer: $x = 0$ or $x = 4$

Example 8: A Harder Trinomial

Solve $2x^2 + 7x + 3 = 0$.

Solution:

This trinomial has $a = 2 \neq 1$, so simple sum-product factoring doesn't apply directly. We use trial and error: the factored form must look like $(2x + \text{?})(x + \text{?})$ where the two unknown numbers multiply to give 3.

The factor pairs of 3 are: $(1)(3)$ or $(3)(1)$.

Try $(2x + 1)(x + 3)$: multiplying out gives $2x^2 + 6x + x + 3 = 2x^2 + 7x + 3$ ✓

That's our factorization. Now apply the Zero Product Property:

$2x + 1 = 0$ gives $x = -\frac{1}{2}$

$x + 3 = 0$ gives $x = -3$

Answer: $x = -\frac{1}{2}$ or $x = -3$

Notice that when $a \neq 1$, fractional solutions are common.

Example 9: A Perfect Square Trinomial

Solve $x^2 - 4x + 4 = 0$.

Solution:

Recognize this as a perfect square: $x^2 - 4x + 4 = (x - 2)^2$.

So the equation becomes $(x - 2)^2 = 0$.

The only way a square can equal zero is if the thing being squared is zero:

$x - 2 = 0$, so $x = 2$

Answer: $x = 2$

This equation has only one solution, but we call it a repeated root or double root because the factor $(x - 2)$ appears twice. Geometrically, this means the parabola $y = x^2 - 4x + 4$ touches the x-axis at exactly one point rather than crossing it. We'll explore this more in Section 16.7.

Example 10: Already Factored but Not Zero

Solve $(x + 1)(x - 3) = 5$.

Solution:

The right side is not zero, so we cannot apply the Zero Product Property directly. We must expand, rearrange to standard form, and factor again.

Expand the left side:

$(x + 1)(x - 3) = x^2 - 3x + x - 3 = x^2 - 2x - 3$

The equation becomes $x^2 - 2x - 3 = 5$.

Subtract 5 to get standard form: $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$.

Factor: we need numbers with product $-8$ and sum $-2$. Those are $-4$ and $2$.

$x^2 - 2x - 8 = (x - 4)(x + 2) = 0$

Apply the Zero Product Property:

$x - 4 = 0$ gives $x = 4$

$x + 2 = 0$ gives $x = -2$

Answer: $x = 4$ or $x = -2$

Check: For $x = 4$: $(4 + 1)(4 - 3) = (5)(1) = 5$ ✓
For $x = -2$: $(-2 + 1)(-2 - 3) = (-1)(-5) = 5$ ✓

When Factoring Doesn't Work Easily

Solving by factoring is efficient—when it works. But not every quadratic factors nicely over the integers.

Consider $x^2 + x - 1 = 0$. We need integers with product $-1$ and sum $1$. The only factor pairs of $-1$ are $(1)(-1)$ and $(-1)(1)$, which sum to $0$ or $0$. Neither works. This quadratic is prime over the integers.

Does this mean the equation has no solutions? Not at all. It just means we need different tools. In Sections 16.4 and 16.5, we'll develop two more methods: completing the square and the quadratic formula. These methods work for any quadratic equation, whether it factors nicely or not.

For now, when a problem says "solve by factoring," it means the equation has been chosen to factor nicely. In real-world applications, you'll typically reach for the quadratic formula unless you spot an obvious factorization.

16.4 Completing the Square

In Section 16.3, we solved quadratic equations by factoring. But not every quadratic factors nicely over the integers. What do we do when faced with an equation like $x^2 + 6x + 7 = 0$, where no integer pair has product 7 and sum 6?

We need a method that works for any quadratic, whether it factors nicely or not. The technique is called completing the square, and it has a beautiful geometric interpretation.

The Geometric Idea

Consider the expression $x^2 + 6x$. We can visualize this as an area: a square with side $x$ (area $x^2$) plus a rectangle with dimensions $x$ by $6$ (area $6x$).

Here's the key insight: if we split that rectangle into two pieces of $3x$ each, we can rearrange them to almost form a larger square. The only thing missing is a small corner piece.

(a) Original: $x^2 + 6x$
(b) Completed: $(x + 3)^2$

Fig. 16.1: Completing the square geometrically. (a) The expression $x^2 + 6x$ as a square plus rectangle. (b) Rearranging and adding 9 to complete a square of side $x + 3$.

The figure shows the key insight: $x^2 + 6x$ is almost a perfect square. If we add 9 (a $3 \times 3$ corner), we get $(x + 3)^2$. In other words:

$x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2$

The number we add—9 in this case—is always the square of half the coefficient of $x$. Here, the coefficient of $x$ is 6, half of that is 3, and $3^2 = 9$.

The Algebraic Procedure

To complete the square on $x^2 + bx$:

Completing the Square

For the expression $x^2 + bx$:

  1. Take half the coefficient of $x$: that's $\frac{b}{2}$
  2. Square it: $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$
  3. Add this value to create a perfect square trinomial:

$x^2 + bx + \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2$

Example 11: Completing the Square (Expression)

Complete the square: $x^2 + 10x + \underline{\phantom{00}} = (\underline{\phantom{00}})^2$

Solution:

The coefficient of $x$ is 10. Half of 10 is 5. Square it: $5^2 = 25$.

$x^2 + 10x + 25 = (x + 5)^2$

Check: $(x + 5)^2 = x^2 + 10x + 25$ ✓

Example 12: Completing the Square (Negative Coefficient)

Complete the square: $x^2 - 8x + \underline{\phantom{00}} = (\underline{\phantom{00}})^2$

Solution:

The coefficient of $x$ is $-8$. Half of $-8$ is $-4$. Square it: $(-4)^2 = 16$.

$x^2 - 8x + 16 = (x - 4)^2$

Check: $(x - 4)^2 = x^2 - 8x + 16$ ✓

Solving Equations by Completing the Square

Now we can use this technique to solve quadratic equations that don't factor nicely.

Example 13: Solving by Completing the Square

Solve $x^2 + 6x + 7 = 0$.

Solution:

This trinomial doesn't factor over integers (no pair has product 7 and sum 6). We'll complete the square instead.

Step 1: Move the constant to the other side.

$x^2 + 6x = -7$

Step 2: Complete the square on the left. Half of 6 is 3, and $3^2 = 9$. Add 9 to both sides to keep the equation balanced.

$x^2 + 6x + 9 = -7 + 9$

$(x + 3)^2 = 2$

Step 3: Take the square root of both sides. Remember: if $u^2 = k$, then $u = \pm\sqrt{k}$.

$x + 3 = \pm\sqrt{2}$

Step 4: Solve for $x$.

$x = -3 \pm \sqrt{2}$

Answer: $x = -3 + \sqrt{2}$ or $x = -3 - \sqrt{2}$

As decimals: $x \approx -3 + 1.414 = -1.586$ or $x \approx -3 - 1.414 = -4.414$

Don't Forget: Add to Both Sides

When you add a number to complete the square, you must add the same number to the other side of the equation. An equation is like a balance scale—whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other.

Example 14: Another Equation

Solve $x^2 - 4x - 1 = 0$.

Solution:

Move the constant: $x^2 - 4x = 1$

Complete the square: half of $-4$ is $-2$, and $(-2)^2 = 4$.

$x^2 - 4x + 4 = 1 + 4$

$(x - 2)^2 = 5$

Take square roots:

$x - 2 = \pm\sqrt{5}$

$x = 2 \pm \sqrt{5}$

Answer: $x = 2 + \sqrt{5} \approx 4.236$ or $x = 2 - \sqrt{5} \approx -0.236$

Example 15: When the Leading Coefficient Isn't 1

Solve $2x^2 + 12x + 14 = 0$.

Solution:

When $a \neq 1$, first divide every term by $a$ to make the leading coefficient 1.

$x^2 + 6x + 7 = 0$

Now proceed as in Example 13. Move the constant:

$x^2 + 6x = -7$

Complete the square (add 9 to both sides):

$(x + 3)^2 = 2$

Take square roots and solve:

$x = -3 \pm \sqrt{2}$

Answer: $x = -3 + \sqrt{2}$ or $x = -3 - \sqrt{2}$

Example 16: A Case with No Real Solutions

Solve $x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0$.

Solution:

Move the constant: $x^2 + 2x = -5$

Complete the square: half of 2 is 1, and $1^2 = 1$.

$x^2 + 2x + 1 = -5 + 1$

$(x + 1)^2 = -4$

Now we need $x + 1 = \pm\sqrt{-4}$. But there is no real number whose square is negative. Squaring any real number gives a non-negative result.

Answer: No real solutions.

This equation has solutions in the complex numbers (involving $i = \sqrt{-1}$), which you'll encounter in more advanced courses. For now, "no real solutions" is the complete answer.

16.5 The Quadratic Formula

Completing the square works for any quadratic equation, but the algebra can be tedious. What if we did the completing-the-square process once, in general terms, and derived a formula that works for all quadratics?

That's exactly what the quadratic formula is: the result of completing the square on the general equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

Deriving the Formula

Start with the general quadratic equation:

$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$     (where $a \neq 0$)

Step 1: Divide everything by $a$ to make the leading coefficient 1.

$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{c}{a} = 0$

Step 2: Move the constant term to the right side.

$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}$

Step 3: Complete the square. The coefficient of $x$ is $\frac{b}{a}$. Half of this is $\frac{b}{2a}$. Squaring: $\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2}$.

Add this to both sides:

$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2} = -\frac{c}{a} + \frac{b^2}{4a^2}$

Step 4: The left side is now a perfect square. Write it in factored form:

$\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = -\frac{c}{a} + \frac{b^2}{4a^2}$

Step 5: Simplify the right side by finding a common denominator ($4a^2$):

$\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = -\frac{4ac}{4a^2} + \frac{b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}$

Step 6: Take the square root of both sides:

$x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

Step 7: Solve for $x$:

$x = -\frac{b}{2a} \pm \frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

The Quadratic Formula

$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

For the equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$.

This single formula solves any quadratic equation. Just identify $a$, $b$, and $c$, substitute them into the formula, and simplify.

A note of caution: as we've seen, not all quadratic equations have real number solutions. If the expression under the square root (the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$) is negative, there are no real solutions. The formula still "works"—it correctly tells us that no real number satisfies the equation. Later, when you learn about complex numbers, you'll find that every quadratic equation has solutions if complex numbers are permitted—and in fact, the quadratic formula we just derived provides the roots of all quadratic equations, whether those roots are real numbers or complex numbers.

Example 17: Using the Quadratic Formula

Solve $x^2 + 5x + 3 = 0$.

Solution:

Identify the coefficients: $a = 1$, $b = 5$, $c = 3$.

Apply the quadratic formula:

$x = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{5^2 - 4(1)(3)}}{2(1)} = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 12}}{2} = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{13}}{2}$

Answer: $x = \frac{-5 + \sqrt{13}}{2} \approx -0.697$   or   $x = \frac{-5 - \sqrt{13}}{2} \approx -4.303$

Example 18: Integer Solutions

Solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$ using the quadratic formula.

Solution:

Here $a = 1$, $b = -5$, $c = 6$.

$x = \frac{-(-5) \pm \sqrt{(-5)^2 - 4(1)(6)}}{2(1)} = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 24}}{2} = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{1}}{2} = \frac{5 \pm 1}{2}$

So $x = \frac{5 + 1}{2} = 3$ or $x = \frac{5 - 1}{2} = 2$.

Answer: $x = 3$ or $x = 2$

Notice this equation does factor: $x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)$. The quadratic formula works even when factoring would be easier—it just takes more computation.

Example 19: Fractional Solutions

Solve $2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0$.

Solution:

Here $a = 2$, $b = -7$, $c = 3$.

$x = \frac{-(-7) \pm \sqrt{(-7)^2 - 4(2)(3)}}{2(2)} = \frac{7 \pm \sqrt{49 - 24}}{4} = \frac{7 \pm \sqrt{25}}{4} = \frac{7 \pm 5}{4}$

So $x = \frac{7 + 5}{4} = \frac{12}{4} = 3$ or $x = \frac{7 - 5}{4} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$.

Answer: $x = 3$ or $x = \frac{1}{2}$

Example 20: Irrational Solutions with Calculator

Solve $3x^2 + 4x - 2 = 0$. Give exact answers and decimal approximations.

Solution:

Here $a = 3$, $b = 4$, $c = -2$.

$x = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 4(3)(-2)}}{2(3)} = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 24}}{6} = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{40}}{6}$

Simplify $\sqrt{40} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 10} = 2\sqrt{10}$:

$x = \frac{-4 \pm 2\sqrt{10}}{6} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{10}}{3}$

Exact answer: $x = \frac{-2 + \sqrt{10}}{3}$ or $x = \frac{-2 - \sqrt{10}}{3}$

Decimal approximations: Since $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$:

$x \approx \frac{-2 + 3.162}{3} \approx 0.387$   or   $x \approx \frac{-2 - 3.162}{3} \approx -1.721$

Calculator Guidance

For equations with messy coefficients, use a calculator to evaluate the formula. The important skills are:

  • Correctly identifying $a$, $b$, and $c$
  • Setting up the formula accurately (watch the signs!)
  • Interpreting the results in context

Don't waste time on tedious arithmetic when a calculator can handle it reliably.

The Long Road to the Quadratic Formula

The quadratic formula we use today is the endpoint of nearly 4,000 years of mathematical development.

Babylonian mathematicians (c. 1800 BCE) could solve quadratic problems, but their approach was geometric and case-by-case. Given a problem like "a square and its side equal 870," they would use cut-and-paste geometry—essentially completing the square with actual squares and rectangles. They had no symbolic algebra and couldn't write a general formula, but they understood the underlying geometric idea.

Greek mathematicians continued the geometric tradition. Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BCE) contains propositions equivalent to solving quadratics, but expressed as relationships between geometric figures rather than equations. The Greeks were uncomfortable with the idea of negative numbers and irrational lengths, which limited how far they could generalize.

Indian mathematicians made crucial advances. Brahmagupta (628 CE) gave explicit rules for solving quadratic equations, including cases with negative coefficients. He accepted negative numbers as legitimate solutions—a conceptual leap the Greeks hadn't made. Sridhara (c. 870 CE) essentially stated the quadratic formula, though in verbal rather than symbolic form.

Islamic mathematicians synthesized and extended these traditions. Al-Khwarizmi (c. 820 CE) wrote the foundational algebra text that gave us the word "algebra." He solved quadratics by completing the square—the same method we used to derive the formula—and gave systematic procedures for each case. His work was translated into Latin and became the basis for European algebra.

The symbolic formula we use today, $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$, required the development of algebraic notation in 16th- and 17th-century Europe. It condenses thousands of years of insight into a single line.

What about higher-degree equations? After the quadratic formula was established, mathematicians sought similar formulas for cubic equations ($ax^3 + \ldots = 0$) and quartic equations ($ax^4 + \ldots = 0$). Both were found in the 16th century by Italian mathematicians. But in 1824, the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel proved something remarkable: there is no general formula using only arithmetic operations and roots for polynomial equations of degree five or higher. Some problems are fundamentally harder than others.

16.6 The Discriminant

In the quadratic formula, the expression under the square root sign determines whether the solutions are real or not, and whether there are two distinct solutions or just one. This expression is important enough to have its own name.

The Discriminant

For the quadratic equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, the discriminant is

$D = b^2 - 4ac$

The discriminant tells us about the nature of the solutions without requiring us to compute them fully.

Since the quadratic formula is $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a}$, the value of $D$ determines what happens:

What the Discriminant Tells Us

  • If $D > 0$: Two distinct real solutions. The square root is a positive number, so $\pm\sqrt{D}$ gives two different values.
  • If $D = 0$: Exactly one real solution (a repeated or double root). The $\pm$ doesn't matter because $\sqrt{0} = 0$, so both cases give $x = \frac{-b}{2a}$.
  • If $D < 0$: No real solutions. The square root of a negative number doesn't exist in the real numbers.

The Geometric Meaning

The discriminant has a beautiful geometric interpretation. In Section 12, we saw that linear equations like $y = mx + b$ produce straight-line graphs. What about quadratic equations?

The graph of $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ is a curve called a parabola—a symmetric U-shaped curve (or upside-down U if $a < 0$). You can plot a parabola by choosing various $x$-values, computing the corresponding $y$-values from the equation, and connecting the resulting points. We'll explore parabolas in detail in Section 16.7, but for now, the key fact is this: the solutions of the equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ are precisely the $x$-values where the parabola crosses the $x$-axis (where $y = 0$).

The discriminant tells us how the parabola meets the $x$-axis.

$D > 0$: Two crossings
$D = 0$: One touching
$D < 0$: No crossing

Example 22: Using the Discriminant

Without solving, determine the number and type of solutions for each equation.

(a) $x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0$

$D = (-6)^2 - 4(1)(9) = 36 - 36 = 0$

Since $D = 0$: one real solution (a double root).

(b) $2x^2 + 3x - 5 = 0$

$D = 3^2 - 4(2)(-5) = 9 + 40 = 49$

Since $D = 49 > 0$: two distinct real solutions.

(Note: $49 = 7^2$ is a perfect square, so these solutions will be rational.)

(c) $3x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0$

$D = (-2)^2 - 4(3)(1) = 4 - 12 = -8$

Since $D = -8 < 0$: no real solutions.

Discriminant Quick Check

Before diving into the full quadratic formula, it's often worth computing the discriminant first:

  • If $D < 0$, there are no real solutions. (If you need only real solutions, you can stop here. However, complex solutions exist and are important in engineering and physics—you'll encounter them in later courses.)
  • If $D$ is a perfect square ($0, 1, 4, 9, 16, \ldots$), the solutions will be rational, and factoring might be easier.
  • If $D > 0$ but not a perfect square, expect irrational solutions.

When to Use Which Method?

We now have three methods for solving quadratic equations: factoring, completing the square, and the quadratic formula. Here's a practical decision guide:

Choosing a Solution Method

Try factoring first if:

  • The coefficients are small integers
  • You quickly spot a factorization
  • The problem specifically asks for factoring

Use the quadratic formula if:

  • Factoring doesn't work quickly
  • The coefficients are large or messy
  • You want to guarantee a solution method that always works

Use completing the square if:

  • You need to convert to vertex form (Section 16.8)
  • The equation is nearly a perfect square already
  • The problem specifically asks for this method

In practice, most people try a quick mental factoring check, then move to the quadratic formula if that doesn't work.

Computing with the Quadratic Formula

The quadratic formula is straightforward to program. If you're comfortable with Python or another programming language, here's a simple implementation: 

import math

def solve_quadratic(a, b, c):
    """
    Solve ax^2 + bx + c = 0
    Returns a tuple of solutions, or a message if no real solutions exist.
    """
    if a == 0:
        return "Not a quadratic equation (a = 0)"
    
    discriminant = b**2 - 4*a*c
    
    if discriminant < 0:
        return "No real solutions (discriminant < 0)"
    elif discriminant == 0:
        x = -b / (2*a)
        return (x,)  # One repeated root
    else:
        sqrt_d = math.sqrt(discriminant)
        x1 = (-b + sqrt_d) / (2*a)
        x2 = (-b - sqrt_d) / (2*a)
        return (x1, x2)

# Example usage:
print(solve_quadratic(1, -5, 6))   # x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0  ->  (3.0, 2.0)
print(solve_quadratic(1, 2, 1))    # x^2 + 2x + 1 = 0  ->  (-1.0,)
print(solve_quadratic(1, 1, 1))    # x^2 + x + 1 = 0   ->  No real solutions

To run this code: Save it as a .py file (e.g., quadratic.py) and run with python3 quadratic.py. Python is free and available at python.org.

The program uses the discriminant to determine which case applies, then computes and returns the appropriate solutions. This is exactly the decision process you'd use by hand, but the computer handles the arithmetic.

16.7 The Graph of a Quadratic Function

In Section 16.6, we previewed the connection between quadratic equations and their graphs. Now let's explore this systematically. Understanding how quadratics look when graphed helps you visualize solutions, check your work, and solve real-world optimization problems.

The Parent Function: $y = x^2$

The simplest quadratic function is $y = x^2$. To graph it, we can build a table of values:

$x$ $-3$ $-2$ $-1$ $0$ $1$ $2$ $3$
$y = x^2$ $9$ $4$ $1$ $0$ $1$ $4$ $9$

Plotting these points and connecting them with a smooth curve gives us a parabola—a symmetric U-shaped curve. Notice that the left and right sides are mirror images of each other.

Key Features of a Parabola

  • Vertex: The lowest (or highest) point on the parabola. For $y = x^2$, the vertex is at $(0, 0)$.
  • Axis of symmetry: The vertical line through the vertex. The parabola is symmetric about this line. For $y = x^2$, the axis of symmetry is $x = 0$ (the y-axis).
  • Opens upward or downward: For $y = x^2$, the parabola opens upward (like a cup that could hold water).

How the Coefficient $a$ Affects the Graph

The general form of a quadratic function is $y = ax^2 + bx + c$. The leading coefficient $a$ controls two important features: the direction the parabola opens and how wide or narrow it is.

Effect of the Coefficient $a$

  • If $a > 0$: The parabola opens upward (U-shape). The vertex is the minimum point.
  • If $a < 0$: The parabola opens downward (∩-shape). The vertex is the maximum point.
  • Larger $|a|$: The parabola is narrower (steeper sides).
  • Smaller $|a|$: The parabola is wider (gentler sides).
(a) $a > 0$: Opens up
(b) $a < 0$: Opens down
(c) Width comparison

Solution:

16.8 Finding the Vertex

The vertex is the most important point on a parabola. It's where the curve turns around—the minimum point if the parabola opens upward, or the maximum point if it opens downward. Many real-world problems come down to finding this critical point.

The Vertex Formula

For the parabola $y = ax^2 + bx + c$, the x-coordinate of the vertex has a simple formula:

Vertex Formula

For $y = ax^2 + bx + c$, the vertex is at:

$x = -\frac{b}{2a}$

To find the y-coordinate, substitute this x-value back into the original equation.

Where does this formula come from? The axis of symmetry passes through the vertex, and by symmetry, it must be exactly halfway between the two x-intercepts (if they exist). Using the quadratic formula, the x-intercepts are $\frac{-b + \sqrt{D}}{2a}$ and $\frac{-b - \sqrt{D}}{2a}$. Their average is:

$\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-b + \sqrt{D}}{2a} + \frac{-b - \sqrt{D}}{2a}\right) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{-2b}{2a} = -\frac{b}{2a}$

The $\pm\sqrt{D}$ terms cancel, leaving $-\frac{b}{2a}$ regardless of whether real roots exist.

Example 24: Finding the Vertex

Find the vertex of $y = x^2 - 6x + 5$.

Solution:

Here $a = 1$, $b = -6$, $c = 5$.

The x-coordinate of the vertex is:

$x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-6}{2(1)} = \frac{6}{2} = 3$

To find the y-coordinate, substitute $x = 3$ into the equation:

$y = (3)^2 - 6(3) + 5 = 9 - 18 + 5 = -4$

Answer: The vertex is at $(3, -4)$.

Since $a = 1 > 0$, the parabola opens upward, so this vertex is a minimum point.

Example 25: Finding the Vertex (Negative $a$)

Find the vertex of $y = -2x^2 + 8x + 3$.

Solution:

Here $a = -2$, $b = 8$, $c = 3$.

$x = -\frac{8}{2(-2)} = -\frac{8}{-4} = 2$

Substitute $x = 2$:

$y = -2(2)^2 + 8(2) + 3 = -8 + 16 + 3 = 11$

Answer: The vertex is at $(2, 11)$.

Since $a = -2 < 0$, the parabola opens downward, so this vertex is a maximum point.

16.9 Graphing Strategy

Now we can put everything together into a systematic approach for graphing quadratic functions.

Graphing a Parabola: Step by Step

  1. Determine the direction: If $a > 0$, opens up. If $a < 0$, opens down.
  2. Find the vertex: Use $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$, then substitute to find $y$.
  3. Find the axis of symmetry: The vertical line $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$ (same as the vertex's x-coordinate).
  4. Find the y-intercept: Set $x = 0$ and evaluate. This gives the point $(0, c)$.
  5. Find the x-intercepts (if any): Set $y = 0$ and solve $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$. Use the discriminant to check if real solutions exist.
  6. Plot and connect: Plot the vertex, intercepts, and a few additional points. Use symmetry to find points on both sides of the vertex. Draw a smooth curve.

16.10 Applications of Quadratic Equations

Quadratic equations arise naturally in many real-world situations. They appear whenever two linear quantities are multiplied together, or when physics involves constant acceleration. This section explores several types of applications.

Projectile Motion

When an object is thrown or launched near Earth's surface, gravity pulls it downward with constant acceleration. This constant acceleration produces a quadratic relationship between height and time.

For vertical motion under gravity (ignoring air resistance), the height at time $t$ is:

Projectile Height Formula

$h(t) = -\frac{1}{2}g t^2 + v_0 t + h_0$

In feet and seconds: $h(t) = -16t^2 + v_0 t + h_0$   (since $g \approx 32$ ft/s²)

In meters and seconds: $h(t) = -4.9t^2 + v_0 t + h_0$   (since $g \approx 9.8$ m/s²)

where $v_0$ is the initial upward velocity and $h_0$ is the initial height.

Understanding why this formula has a $t^2$ term is important. Problems involving motion are one of the main reasons calculus was invented—calculus is the natural language for describing how things change. But we can understand the key ideas with careful reasoning, even without calculus.

Understanding Motion with Constant Acceleration

Challenge: Can we derive the projectile formula using only basic mathematical reasoning? Yes—by breaking the problem into two parts.

Part 1: A World Without Gravity (Inertia)

Imagine a universe with no gravity. You stand on a platform at height $h_0$ and throw a ball upward with speed $v_0$. What happens? The ball never slows down—it just keeps going at the same speed forever. This is Newton's first law: objects in motion stay in motion unless a force acts on them.

In this gravity-free world, distance = rate × time, so the height at time $t$ would be:

$h = h_0 + v_0 \cdot t$

Part 2: Gravity Alone (The Drop)

Now imagine a different scenario: you hold a ball at some height and simply let go (initial velocity is zero). How far does it fall in $t$ seconds?

Gravity doesn't just move the ball—it accelerates it, adding about $g = 32$ ft/s (or $9.8$ m/s) to its downward speed every second. So after $t$ seconds, the ball is moving at speed $g \cdot t$.

Here's the key question: if the speed keeps changing, how do we calculate distance? We can't use starting speed (zero) or ending speed ($g \cdot t$)—neither represents the whole trip.

The answer: use the average speed. Because gravity increases speed smoothly (linearly), the average speed over the interval is exactly halfway between start and finish:

Average speed $= \frac{0 + gt}{2} = \frac{gt}{2}$

Distance fallen $=$ average speed $\times$ time $= \frac{gt}{2} \cdot t = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$

Part 3: Combining Both Effects

In the real world, both effects happen simultaneously: the ball tries to fly upward (inertia) while gravity pulls it down. We simply add the two contributions:

$h(t) = h_0 + v_0 t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$

The negative sign appears because gravity pulls down (decreasing height). With $g = 32$ ft/s², we get $\frac{1}{2}g = 16$, giving us the formula $h(t) = -16t^2 + v_0 t + h_0$.

Since the coefficient of $t^2$ is negative, the parabola opens downward. This matches physical intuition: the projectile rises, reaches a maximum height at the vertex, then falls.

Connecting to What We've Learned

Maximum height: The vertex of the parabola (use $t = -b/(2a)$ from Section 16.8)

When it hits the ground: Solve $h(t) = 0$ for $t > 0$ (the positive x-intercept, after launch)

How high at a specific time: Substitute that $t$ value into the formula

For projectiles launched at an angle, the motion has both vertical and horizontal components. The vertical motion follows the quadratic formula above; the horizontal motion (ignoring air resistance) is simply distance = rate × time, with no acceleration.

Note: In practice, you'd often know the launch angle and total speed rather than separate velocity components. Converting between these requires trigonometry, which you'll learn later. For now, we'll work directly with horizontal and vertical velocity components.

Example 28: Projectile with Horizontal Motion

A ball is launched from a platform 12 feet high with an initial upward velocity of 48 ft/s and an initial horizontal velocity of 30 ft/s.

(a) When does the ball hit the ground?

(b) What is the maximum height?

(c) How far does the ball travel horizontally?

Solution:

The height function is $h(t) = -16t^2 + 48t + 12$, where:

  • The $-16t^2$ term comes from gravity: $-\frac{1}{2}g = -\frac{1}{2}(32) = -16$
  • The $48t$ term reflects the initial upward velocity
  • The $12$ is the initial height (the platform)

(a) When does it hit the ground?

We need to find when $h(t) = 0$ for $t > 0$ (after launch at $t = 0$):

$-16t^2 + 48t + 12 = 0$

Divide by $-4$ to simplify: $4t^2 - 12t - 3 = 0$

Using the quadratic formula with $a = 4$, $b = -12$, $c = -3$:

$t = \frac{12 \pm \sqrt{144 + 48}}{8} = \frac{12 \pm \sqrt{192}}{8} = \frac{12 \pm 8\sqrt{3}}{8} = \frac{3 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{2}$

This gives $t \approx 3.23$ seconds or $t \approx -0.23$ seconds. The negative value corresponds to a time before launch (when the ball would have been at ground level if the motion extended backward in time). We want the positive value: $t \approx 3.23$ seconds.

(b) Maximum height:

The vertex occurs at $t = -\frac{48}{2(-16)} = \frac{48}{32} = 1.5$ seconds.

Height at $t = 1.5$:

$h(1.5) = -16(1.5)^2 + 48(1.5) + 12 = -36 + 72 + 12 = 48$ feet

(c) Horizontal distance:

Horizontal motion has no acceleration, so: distance = rate × time

$d = 30 \times 3.23 \approx 96.9$ feet

Answer: (a) About 3.23 seconds; (b) 48 feet; (c) About 96.9 feet

Let's compare this trajectory with a different launch. Suppose a second ball is launched from a 6-foot platform with an upward velocity of 40 ft/s and horizontal velocity of 25 ft/s.

Second trajectory: $h(t) = -16t^2 + 40t + 6$

  • Landing time: Solve $-16t^2 + 40t + 6 = 0$, giving $t \approx 2.64$ seconds
  • Maximum height: At $t = 40/32 = 1.25$ s, height $= -16(1.5625) + 50 + 6 = 31$ feet
  • Horizontal distance: $25 \times 2.64 \approx 66$ feet

Computing with Python

Here's a Python program that computes projectile motion. You provide the horizontal and vertical velocity components and the launch height; it calculates time of flight, maximum height, and horizontal range. 

import math

def projectile_motion(v_horizontal, v_vertical, launch_height):
    """
    Calculate projectile trajectory (using feet and seconds).
    
    v_horizontal: initial horizontal velocity (ft/s)
    v_vertical: initial upward velocity (ft/s)  
    launch_height: height of launch point (ft)
    
    Returns: (time_of_flight, max_height, horizontal_range)
    """
    # Height equation: h(t) = -16t^2 + v_vertical*t + launch_height
    # The -16 comes from (1/2)*g where g = 32 ft/s^2
    
    # Solve -16t^2 + v_vertical*t + launch_height = 0 for landing time
    a = -16
    b = v_vertical
    c = launch_height
    
    discriminant = b**2 - 4*a*c
    if discriminant < 0:
        return "Error: projectile never reaches ground"
    
    # Take the positive root (landing time, after launch)
    t_flight = (-b - math.sqrt(discriminant)) / (2*a)
    
    # Maximum height at vertex: t = -b/(2a) = v_vertical/32
    t_max = v_vertical / 32
    max_height = -16*t_max**2 + v_vertical*t_max + launch_height
    
    # Horizontal range (no acceleration horizontally)
    horizontal_range = v_horizontal * t_flight
    
    return (t_flight, max_height, horizontal_range)

# Example from the lesson (Ball 1):
result = projectile_motion(30, 48, 12)
print(f"Time of flight: {result[0]:.2f} seconds")
print(f"Maximum height: {result[1]:.2f} feet")
print(f"Horizontal range: {result[2]:.2f} feet")

# Try Ball 2:
result2 = projectile_motion(25, 40, 6)
print(f"\nBall 2: {result2[0]:.2f} s, {result2[1]:.2f} ft max, {result2[2]:.2f} ft range")

Area Optimization

A classic application of quadratics is finding maximum or minimum values. Because the vertex of a parabola is either the highest or lowest point on the curve, quadratics are perfectly suited for optimization problems.

Example 29: Maximizing Enclosed Area

A farmer has 120 feet of fencing to build a rectangular pen against an existing barn wall. Only three sides need fencing (the barn forms the fourth side). What dimensions maximize the enclosed area?

Fig. 16.7: Rectangular pen against a barn wall. Only three sides require fencing.

Solution:

Let $w$ be the width (the two sides perpendicular to the barn). The fencing for these two sides uses $2w$ feet, leaving $120 - 2w$ feet for the side parallel to the barn.

The area is:

$A(w) = w(120 - 2w) = 120w - 2w^2 = -2w^2 + 120w$

This is a quadratic with $a = -2 < 0$, so the parabola opens downward and has a maximum at its vertex.

The vertex occurs at:

$w = -\frac{120}{2(-2)} = \frac{120}{4} = 30$ feet

When $w = 30$: length $= 120 - 2(30) = 60$ feet.

Maximum area $= 30 \times 60 = 1800$ square feet.

Answer: Width 30 feet, length 60 feet, for a maximum area of 1800 square feet.

Notice the pattern: the problem gives a constraint (fixed amount of fencing), which creates a relationship between variables. Substituting this relationship into the area formula produces a quadratic, and the vertex gives the optimal value.

Revenue Problems

Revenue equals price times quantity sold: $R = p \cdot q$. In many situations, raising the price decreases the quantity sold (and vice versa). If both price and quantity are linear functions of some variable, their product is quadratic.

Example 30: Maximizing Revenue

A theater currently charges 8 dollars per ticket and sells 400 tickets per show. Market research indicates that for each 0.50 dollar increase in price, 20 fewer tickets will be sold. What ticket price maximizes revenue?

Solution:

Let $x$ = number of 0.50 dollar price increases.

Then: Price $= 8 + 0.5x$ dollars per ticket

Tickets sold $= 400 - 20x$

Revenue:

$R(x) = (8 + 0.5x)(400 - 20x)$

Expand:

$R(x) = 3200 - 160x + 200x - 10x^2 = -10x^2 + 40x + 3200$

The vertex occurs at:

$x = -\frac{40}{2(-10)} = \frac{40}{20} = 2$

So 2 price increases of 0.50 dollars each means: Price $= 8 + 0.5(2) = 9$ dollars.

Maximum revenue $= -10(4) + 40(2) + 3200 = -40 + 80 + 3200 = 3240$ dollars.

Check: At 9 dollars, tickets sold $= 400 - 20(2) = 360$. Revenue $= 9 \times 360 = 3240$ dollars. ✓

Answer: Ticket price of 9 dollars maximizes revenue at 3240 dollars per show.

Geometric Problems

Many geometry problems lead to quadratic equations. Problems involving area (which multiplies two dimensions) or the Pythagorean theorem (which squares lengths) often produce quadratics.

Example 31: Finding Dimensions from Area

The length of a rectangle is 5 cm more than its width. If the area is 126 cm², find the dimensions.

Solution:

Let $w$ = width. Then length $= w + 5$.

Area: $w(w + 5) = 126$

$w^2 + 5w = 126$

$w^2 + 5w - 126 = 0$

Try to factor: we need two numbers that multiply to $-126$ and add to $5$.

Since $126 = 2 \times 63 = 2 \times 9 \times 7 = 14 \times 9$, try $14$ and $-9$:   $14 \times (-9) = -126$ and $14 + (-9) = 5$. ✓

$(w + 14)(w - 9) = 0$

So $w = -14$ or $w = 9$. Since width must be positive, $w = 9$ cm.

Length $= 9 + 5 = 14$ cm.

Check: $9 \times 14 = 126$ cm². ✓

Answer: Width 9 cm, length 14 cm.

Example 32: Pythagorean Theorem Application

The legs of a right triangle differ by 3 cm. If the hypotenuse is 15 cm, find the lengths of the legs.

Solution:

Let the shorter leg be $x$. Then the longer leg is $x + 3$.

By the Pythagorean theorem: $x^2 + (x + 3)^2 = 15^2$

$x^2 + x^2 + 6x + 9 = 225$

$2x^2 + 6x + 9 = 225$

$2x^2 + 6x - 216 = 0$

Divide by 2: $x^2 + 3x - 108 = 0$

Try to factor: need two numbers that multiply to $-108$ and add to $3$.

Since $108 = 12 \times 9$, try $12$ and $-9$:   $12 \times (-9) = -108$ and $12 + (-9) = 3$. ✓

$(x + 12)(x - 9) = 0$

So $x = -12$ or $x = 9$. Since length must be positive, $x = 9$ cm.

The legs are 9 cm and 12 cm.

Check: $9^2 + 12^2 = 81 + 144 = 225 = 15^2$. ✓

Answer: The legs are 9 cm and 12 cm.

Circuit Problems

Quadratic equations also appear in electronics. When resistors are connected in parallel, the combined resistance follows a formula that can lead to quadratic equations.

Resistors in Parallel

When two resistors with resistances $R_1$ and $R_2$ are wired in parallel, the total resistance $R$ satisfies:

$\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$

The total resistance is always less than either individual resistor. (Think of it as giving electricity more paths to flow through.)

Fig. 16.8: Two resistors wired in parallel. Current can flow through either path.

Example 33: Finding Resistor Values

You're building a circuit that requires a total resistance of 2 ohms. You have a collection of resistors in multiples of 3 ohms: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, ... ohms. Can you achieve 2 ohms by wiring two of these resistors in parallel?

Solution:

We want to find resistor values from our collection that combine to give 2 ohms total. Let's check if two consecutive sizes (differing by 3 ohms) can work.

Let $R_1 = x$ and $R_2 = x + 3$. We need:

$\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{x + 3}$

Multiply both sides by $2x(x + 3)$:

$x(x + 3) = 2(x + 3) + 2x$

$x^2 + 3x = 2x + 6 + 2x$

$x^2 + 3x = 4x + 6$

$x^2 - x - 6 = 0$

Factor: $(x - 3)(x + 2) = 0$

So $x = 3$ or $x = -2$. Since resistance must be positive, $x = 3$ ohms.

The two resistors are $R_1 = 3$ ohms and $R_2 = 6$ ohms.

Check: $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$, so $R = 2$ ohms. ✓

Answer: Yes! Wire a 3-ohm resistor in parallel with a 6-ohm resistor.

16.11 Section Summary

This section covered quadratic equations from multiple angles: what they are, how to solve them, how to graph them, and where they appear in applications. Here's a consolidated reference.

Key Formulas for Quadratic Equations

Standard Form:   $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$   (where $a \neq 0$)

Quadratic Formula:   $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$

Discriminant:   $D = b^2 - 4ac$

$D > 0$: two real solutions   |   $D = 0$: one real solution   |   $D < 0$: no real solutions

Vertex of $y = ax^2 + bx + c$:   $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$   (then substitute to find $y$)

Parabola Direction:   Opens up if $a > 0$ (vertex is minimum); opens down if $a < 0$ (vertex is maximum)

Sign of $a$ Parabola opens up ($a > 0$) or down ($a < 0$)

Looking Ahead

Quadratic equations are foundational for much of mathematics:

Section 17 (Functions) will formalize the relationship between equations and their graphs. The parabola $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ is an example of a quadratic function, and you'll learn about function notation, domain, range, and transformations.

Trigonometry uses quadratic equations frequently. Many trig identities lead to quadratics, and solving triangles often requires quadratic techniques.

Calculus provides another way to find vertices: the derivative of a function equals zero at maximum and minimum points. For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$, the derivative is $f'(x) = 2ax + b$, and setting this to zero gives $x = -b/(2a)$—the same vertex formula, derived by a completely different method.

Science and Engineering are full of quadratic relationships: projectile motion, energy equations, optimization problems, circuit analysis, and statistical curve fitting all use the techniques you've learned here.

Solutions

50. Ball thrown straight up, $v_0 = 64$ ft/s from ground level.

Height function: $h(t) = -16t^2 + 64t$

(a) Max height at $t = -\frac{64}{2(-16)} = 2$ seconds

(b) $h(2) = -16(4) + 64(2) = -64 + 128 = 64$ feet

(c) Returns when $h(t) = 0$: $-16t^2 + 64t = 0 \Rightarrow -16t(t - 4) = 0 \Rightarrow t = 0$ or $t = 4$

Answer: (a) 2 seconds; (b) 64 feet; (c) 4 seconds

51. Stone dropped from 80 feet.

(a) $h(t) = -16t^2 + 80$ (initial velocity is 0)

(b) Solve $-16t^2 + 80 = 0$:

$16t^2 = 80 \Rightarrow t^2 = 5 \Rightarrow t = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$ seconds

Answer: (a) $h(t) = -16t^2 + 80$; (b) $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$ seconds

52. Projectile: $h_0 = 5$ m, $v_{0y} = 25$ m/s, $v_{0x} = 15$ m/s.

Height: $h(t) = -4.9t^2 + 25t + 5$

(a) Max height at $t = \frac{25}{9.8} \approx 2.551$ seconds

$h(2.551) = -4.9(6.508) + 25(2.551) + 5 \approx -31.89 + 63.78 + 5 = 36.89$ meters

(b) Solve $-4.9t^2 + 25t + 5 = 0$:

$t = \frac{-25 \pm \sqrt{625 + 98}}{-9.8} = \frac{-25 \pm \sqrt{723}}{-9.8} = \frac{-25 \pm 26.89}{-9.8}$

Take $t = \frac{-25 - 26.89}{-9.8} = \frac{-51.89}{-9.8} \approx 5.30$ seconds

(c) Horizontal distance: $15 \times 5.30 \approx 79.5$ meters

Answer: (a) About 36.9 meters; (b) About 5.30 seconds; (c) About 79.5 meters

53. 200 feet of fencing, four sides.

Let $w$ = width. Perimeter: $2w + 2\ell = 200 \Rightarrow \ell = 100 - w$

Area: $A = w(100 - w) = -w^2 + 100w$

Vertex at $w = \frac{100}{2} = 50$ feet. Then $\ell = 50$ feet.

Answer: 50 ft × 50 ft (a square) with area 2500 sq ft

54. 180 meters, three sides (river on fourth).

Let $w$ = width. Then $2w + \ell = 180 \Rightarrow \ell = 180 - 2w$

Area: $A = w(180 - 2w) = -2w^2 + 180w$

Vertex at $w = \frac{180}{4} = 45$ meters. Then $\ell = 180 - 90 = 90$ meters.

Answer: 45 m × 90 m with area 4050 m²

55. 400 feet, three sides (building on fourth).

Let $w$ = width (perpendicular to building). Then $2w + \ell = 400 \Rightarrow \ell = 400 - 2w$

Area: $A = w(400 - 2w) = -2w^2 + 400w$

Vertex at $w = \frac{400}{4} = 100$ feet. Then $\ell = 200$ feet.

Answer: 100 ft × 200 ft with area 20,000 sq ft

56. Bookstore: 150 copies at 20 dollars; each 1 dollar decrease adds 10 sales.

Let $x$ = number of 1 dollar decreases.

Price $= 20 - x$;   Copies $= 150 + 10x$

Revenue: $R = (20 - x)(150 + 10x) = 3000 + 200x - 150x - 10x^2 = -10x^2 + 50x + 3000$

Vertex at $x = \frac{50}{20} = 2.5$

Price $= 20 - 2.5 = 17.50$ dollars

Answer: 17.50 dollars per book maximizes revenue

57. Software: 200 units at 50 dollars; each 5 dollar decrease adds 25 sales.

Let $x$ = number of 5 dollar decreases.

Price $= 50 - 5x$;   Units $= 200 + 25x$

Revenue: $R = (50 - 5x)(200 + 25x) = 10000 + 1250x - 1000x - 125x^2 = -125x^2 + 250x + 10000$

Vertex at $x = \frac{250}{250} = 1$

Price $= 50 - 5(1) = 45$ dollars

Max revenue $= -125 + 250 + 10000 = 10125$ dollars

Answer: 45 dollars per unit maximizes revenue at 10,125 dollars/month

58. Rectangle: length = $2w + 3$, area = 77 m².

$w(2w + 3) = 77 \Rightarrow 2w^2 + 3w - 77 = 0$

Using quadratic formula: $w = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 616}}{4} = \frac{-3 \pm 25}{4}$

$w = \frac{22}{4} = 5.5$ m (reject negative). Length $= 2(5.5) + 3 = 14$ m.

Check: $5.5 \times 14 = 77$ m² ✓

Answer: Width 5.5 m, length 14 m

59. Right triangle with consecutive even integer legs, hypotenuse 10.

Let legs be $x$ and $x + 2$.

$x^2 + (x + 2)^2 = 100$

$x^2 + x^2 + 4x + 4 = 100$

$2x^2 + 4x - 96 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2 + 2x - 48 = 0$

$(x + 8)(x - 6) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 6$ (reject $-8$)

Check: $6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2$ ✓

Answer: The legs are 6 cm and 8 cm

60. Photo: width $w$, length $w + 4$. With 2-inch border on all sides, total dimensions are $(w + 4)$ by $(w + 8)$. Total area = 260 in².

$(w + 4)(w + 8) = 260$

$w^2 + 12w + 32 = 260$

$w^2 + 12w - 228 = 0$

Using quadratic formula: $w = \frac{-12 \pm \sqrt{144 + 912}}{2} = \frac{-12 \pm \sqrt{1056}}{2} = \frac{-12 \pm 32.50}{2}$

$w \approx 10.25$ inches (reject negative). Length $\approx 14.25$ inches.

Check: Total: $(10.25 + 4)(10.25 + 8) = 14.25 \times 18.25 \approx 260$ ✓

Answer: Photo is approximately 10.25 in × 14.25 in

61. Need 6 ohms total, resistors in multiples of 5 ohms, consecutive sizes (differ by 5).

Let $R_1 = x$ and $R_2 = x + 5$.

$\frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{x + 5}$

Multiply by $6x(x + 5)$:

$x(x + 5) = 6(x + 5) + 6x$

$x^2 + 5x = 6x + 30 + 6x$

$x^2 + 5x = 12x + 30$

$x^2 - 7x - 30 = 0$

Factor: $(x - 10)(x + 3) = 0$

$x = 10$ or $x = -3$. Since resistance must be positive, $x = 10$ ohms.

Check: $\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{15} = \frac{3}{30} + \frac{2}{30} = \frac{5}{30} = \frac{1}{6}$, so $R = 6$ ohms. ✓

Answer: Use a 10-ohm resistor and a 15-ohm resistor in parallel.
